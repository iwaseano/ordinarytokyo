**About the phrases in this guide:** every phrase below comes as a card. If pronouncing Japanese feels like too much, just open this page and show the card to the staff — you'll be understood. And if you do try saying it out loud, however it comes out, they'll be even happier that you tried.

Tokyo's trains carry millions of people a day and still feel calm. That calm is not an accident, and it is not enforced by staff. It runs on a set of unwritten rules that every regular follows without thinking: where to stand, when to be silent, how to make room. None of the rules are hard, and most of them are visible if you watch one platform for two minutes. This page walks through one ordinary ride — arriving, lining up, boarding, riding, and getting off — with the manners that matter at each step, and the few phrases that solve almost every problem at a station. One boundary: this is a guide to city trains — JR East's local lines, the subways, and the private railways. The Shinkansen runs on its own rules (its own gates, tickets, and seat customs) and gets a separate guide.

## The ride, step by step

1. **Tap in, and keep your card out.** You tap your IC card (or scan your ticket) at the gate on the way in and again on the way out — the fare is calculated between the two taps. Keep the card reachable; you will need it again.
2. **Follow the floor, not the crowd — and follow the color.** Every line in Tokyo has its own color, and the color is a complete wayfinding system: it runs through the signs, the route maps, the platform markings, and the stripe along the train itself. The Yamanote is light green, the Ginza subway line is orange, and so on — JR lines and subways alike. Once you know your line's color, you can navigate a huge station by color alone, without reading a word. Platforms are painted with those colors, direction arrows, and boarding marks that show exactly where each door will stop, and the signs above give the direction (named by a major station down the line) and the minutes to the next train.
3. **Line up at the door marks.** This is the heart of the system, and the next section covers it in full.
4. **Let everyone off first.** When the train arrives, stand to the side of the door opening, wait for the full stream of passengers to exit, then board in line order.
5. **Move in, set your phone to silent, and hold your bag in front or between your feet.** Details below.
6. **Listen for your station — and watch the displays.** Station names are announced and shown in Japanese and English on the in-car screens, with a diagram of which side the doors open.
7. **Tap out at your destination.** If the fare needs adjusting or your card errors at the gate, use the manned gate at the side and show the card to the attendant — that is a normal, everyday event, not a problem.

## Manners and what not to do

### Line up on the platform — always

Look down: the platform floor has markings where each door stops, with two short lines forming a waiting lane on either side. Passengers queue in those lanes, in single file, leaving the center of the door clear. When the train stops, the exiting passengers walk out through the middle gap, and the two lanes board in turn.

**Do not:**
- **Stand in front of the doors.** The center is the exit path. Standing there blocks a full train car of people and earns you a quiet, collective stare.
- **Skip the line because your lane looks slow.** Lines move fast and in order. Cutting in is rare in Tokyo and very visible.
- **Crowd the yellow tactile paving.** The textured yellow line along the platform edge is a safety guide for visually impaired passengers. Stand behind it, and do not park luggage on it.
- **Run for a train that is about to leave.** Even if you could *just* make it — do not run. Not on the platform, not down the stairs, and never at a closing door. On Tokyo's city lines the next train comes in under five minutes, usually much less. The run risks you, everyone you dodge, and the departure time of a train carrying a thousand people; the wait costs you almost nothing. Locals who miss a train shrug and check their phones. That is the correct move.

### Quiet is the default inside the car

- **No phone calls.** Set your phone to silent — in Japan this is called "manner mode," and you will see the phrase on posters everywhere. If a call comes in, decline it and call back after you get off. Near the priority seats, the stricter custom is to switch the phone off entirely, and signs still ask for that.
- **Mind the sound leaking from your earphones.** Music and videos with earphones are normal; audio leaking out is not. If you can hear your music with an earphone out, so can your neighbor.
- **Conversations are fine — at low volume.** Tokyo trains are not silent libraries, but the volume setting is "the people next to you," not "the whole car." Morning and late-night cars run especially quiet, because half the car is half asleep.

### Cameras stay put away inside the car

Do not take photos or video inside the train. Not of the view, not of the unusual advertisement, not of the one-in-a-million sight outside the window — inside the car, the camera stays away, no matter how rare what you are looking at is. The reason is the people around you: a raised camera in a shared car points at strangers who never agreed to be in anyone's footage, and being filmed by a fellow passenger is one of the fastest ways to make a whole car uncomfortable. Filming is treated as a problem even when your subject was "just the window." Train photography is a real and welcome hobby in Japan — but it happens from the platform and the station, outside the car, never from a seat inside it.

### The doorway is not your space

The area just inside the doors belongs to the flow, not to anyone standing in it. If you ride by the door — a natural spot on a short trip — you have one job at every stop: keep the flow moving. When a crowd gets off or on, the regulars' move is to **step off onto the platform once, let the stream pass, and board again**. It feels counterintuitive the first time, but it is standard practice, nobody will take your "spot," and the train will not leave without you while passengers are still boarding. Planting yourself in the doorway and making forty people squeeze around you is the opposite of the system.

### Never agree to meet at "the station"

"The station" is not a meeting place in Tokyo — it is a small district. The major terminals are enormous: Shinjuku, Tokyo, Ikebukuro, and Ueno stations each span several floors, multiple train companies, and dozens of exits, and two people "at the station" can be a ten-minute walk apart underground. Agreeing to meet at a bare station name is how people lose half an hour and each other. Always pin the meeting point to something specific: a named gate, a numbered exit, or a landmark inside the station — and if you are the one waiting, share your phone's live location too.

### Priority seats are a system, not a suggestion

The seats near the doors with the priority sign are reserved by custom for elderly passengers, people with disabilities, pregnant passengers, and adults with infants. If you sit in one, the expectation is that you watch for those passengers and give the seat up immediately and gracefully. Many locals avoid these seats entirely on busy lines so they never have to judge. If you offer your seat, do it with a gesture and a stand — no announcement needed.

### Backpacks come off your back

In a crowded car, a backpack worn on the back takes up a second person's space and swings into people every time you turn. Tokyo regulars hold their bag in front of them, or set it between their feet, or put it on the overhead rack. Any of the three is fine — and if your bag is heavy, or you are carrying more than one, the overhead rack is the recommended choice: it frees your hands, your front, and the floor all at once. Leaving it on your back in a packed car is the single most common tourist tell on the morning commute.

### Escalators: left side in Tokyo

On station escalators in Tokyo, standers stand on the **left** and the right side is left open for people walking. This is the reverse of Osaka, which is a famous domestic culture shock even for Japanese people. Two recent changes are worth knowing: many stations now post signs asking people to stand on both sides and not walk, for safety, and compliance is growing. Copy the escalator in front of you: if everyone is standing two-abreast, do the same; if a walking lane has formed on the right, keep left.

### Big luggage

Smaller bags go up on the overhead rack — that is what it is for, and it is the recommended place whenever you are carrying a lot. And do not worry about leaving things up there: theft from an overhead rack is something you simply never hear of in Japan, and regulars ride with their bags overhead and their minds elsewhere. Suitcases too big or heavy for the rack go between your legs or tight against the wall of the car, never across the doorway and never in the aisle where the door stream flows. One exception to know: trains built for airport access — the Narita Express, the Keisei Skyliner, and their kin — have dedicated luggage spaces near the doors or at the ends of the car. On those trains, big suitcases go in the luggage space, not the rack and not your feet. On very crowded rush-hour trains, a large suitcase is genuinely difficult — if your hotel is a few stops away at 8 a.m., a taxi or the hotel luggage-forwarding service will save you and everyone around you a hard ride. And like everywhere in Japan: no tipping for the attendant who helps you, and no eating a full meal on a local train — a sealed drink is fine, a burger is not.

## What locals actually value here

Ask a Tokyo regular what they like about the trains and they rarely mention speed. They mention that it is *predictable*: the train comes when the sign says, the line moves in order, the car is quiet enough to read or sleep, and a dropped glove gets turned in to the station office. The manners are the mechanism. Lining up is not politeness theater — it is how forty people board through one door in twenty seconds. The quiet is not coldness — it is shared rest in a city that runs long hours. Visitors who pick up just the platform queue and the silent phone have already adopted the two rules locals notice most.
