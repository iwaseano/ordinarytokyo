Ask a Tokyo office worker where they eat lunch and a beef bowl chain will come up within the first three answers. Gyudon — thin-sliced beef and onion simmered in a sweet soy broth, served over a bowl of rice — is Japan's original fast food: ordered in seconds, eaten in minutes, and priced so a full meal costs about the same as a coffee in most Western capitals.

Three chains dominate, and locals do not treat them as interchangeable. People have a chain the way they have a football team. This page compares Yoshinoya, Matsuya, and Sukiya — plus Nakau, the fourth name that quietly covers oyakodon and udon — on the things that actually differ: how you order, what you get for the base price, and which one fits which moment.

## First, what is donburi?

*Donburi* is the family all of these bowls belong to, and the word means two things at once: the deep, wide bowl itself, and the dish served in it — steamed white rice with something cooked placed on top.

The design is deliberate. The topping is almost always saucy, and the sauce is meant to soak down into the top layer of rice, so every bite carries both. You eat with chopsticks (a spoon appears for some bowls), and lifting the bowl with one hand is normal table manners here, not a shortcut. A donburi is a complete meal in one vessel: protein, rice, and sauce, usually with miso soup and pickles beside it, at a price and speed built for a working lunch.

Once you see the pattern, the whole family reads easily:

- **Gyudon** — beef and onion simmered in sweet soy dashi (this page)
- **Katsudon** — a pork cutlet simmered with egg
- **Oyakodon** — chicken and egg, literally "parent and child"
- **Tendon** — tempura over rice with a sweet sauce
- **Kaisendon** — raw seafood over rice, the celebratory end of the family
- **Unadon** — grilled eel with a rich glaze

Chains specialize. The chains below compete inside one branch of the family — but the bowl underneath never changes.

## What is gyudon?

If you have never seen one, picture this: a deep bowl of steamed white rice, covered completely with thin slices of beef and soft onion that have been simmered together in a sweet-salty soy broth — soy sauce, mirin, sugar, and dashi. The broth soaks into the top layer of rice, the beef is tender rather than seared, and the onions have gone sweet and translucent. It is eaten with chopsticks, fast, often with a spoonful of bright-red pickled ginger on top and a raw or soft egg mixed in if you like. There is no sauce on the side and nothing to assemble: the bowl arrives finished.

Gyudon is not a modern invention. It grew out of the beef hot pots that became popular in Tokyo in the Meiji era (late 1800s), when eating beef itself was still new in Japan. Street stalls started serving the same simmered beef over rice instead of in a pot — cheaper, faster, and filling enough for laborers and students. Yoshinoya, founded in 1899 at a Tokyo fish market, turned that stall food into a system: one bowl, cooked in big batches, served in minutes. The dish has stayed working-class food ever since. Nobody dresses up for gyudon, nobody books it, and that is exactly the point — it is the meal Tokyo eats when it is not thinking about meals.

## When locals crave it

Gyudon is chosen by mood and situation more than by appetite for beef specifically. The patterns are so consistent that locals recognize themselves in them:

- **The rushed lunch.** Twenty minutes between meetings, a station nearby, no time to decide. Gyudon is the default answer because the decision was made years ago — you walk in already knowing your order.
- **After overtime, late at night.** It is past ten, everything else is closing, and you want something hot, salty, and substantial before the train home. A beef bowl at a counter, eaten in silence among other tired people, is a Tokyo ritual.
- **When money is tight but standards are not.** At the end of the month, or on a student budget, gyudon is how you eat a real, hot, cooked meal — meat, rice, sometimes soup — for the price of a convenience-store sandwich.
- **The morning after, or before a long day.** Chains open early or around the clock, and a beef bowl with a raw egg is a working breakfast for people who skip the pastry idea entirely.
- **Pure habit and comfort.** Some people simply have a standing order — same chain, same size, tsuyudaku — and the craving is for that exact, known bowl. It is comfort food in the truest sense: predictable, warm, and unchanged.

If your mood is "hot, fast, cheap, and no thinking," you are in a gyudon mood. The only remaining question is which chain — and that is what the rest of this page is for.

## Yoshinoya: the original

Yoshinoya is the oldest of the four and the one that defined the dish. The menu is deliberately narrow: the beef bowl in a few sizes, a handful of set combinations, and not much else. The interior is a long counter built for turnover.

**The flow.** Sit down, order verbally at the counter, eat, then pay at the register by the counter when you are done. Water or tea is self-service or placed in front of you. There is no machine and no tablet — a person takes your order, which makes Yoshinoya the easiest first stop if ticket machines worry you.

**What to know.** Because you pay afterwards, the phrase "okaikei o onegai shimasu" (the bill, please) is genuinely useful here. Regulars customize quietly: "tsuyudaku" for extra sauce is the famous one.

## Matsuya: the ticket machine, and soup with everything

Matsuya calls its bowl "gyumeshi" and runs the most systematic operation of the four. Its quiet superpower is that miso soup is included with most eat-in meals — order a beef bowl and a small bowl of soup simply arrives, which makes the same headline price stretch further.

**The flow.** Buy a meal ticket from the machine at the entrance *before* you sit down (cash works everywhere; newer machines take IC cards). Take the ticket, sit, and hand it to staff or place it on the counter. Food arrives; there is nothing to pay afterwards.

**What to know.** The machine is the only hurdle. Decide what you want from the menu board before you reach it, because the lunch line forms right behind you. If you are stuck, the beef bowl button is the big one with the photo near the top.

## Sukiya: the most options, the longest hours

Sukiya is the largest of the four and the most varied. The base gyudon is a starting point for a long list of toppings — three-cheese gyudon is a permanent favorite — plus curry, breakfast sets, and kids' options. Stores lean toward table seating, and many run 24 hours.

**The flow.** Sit first. Order on the table tablet where there is one, or call staff with a raised hand and "sumimasen". Payment is at the register, commonly as you leave. Of the four, this feels least like a conveyor belt and most like a casual restaurant.

**What to know.** Sukiya is where locals bring children and where the night-shift meal happens at 3 a.m. If your group cannot agree on one dish, this is the chain that absorbs disagreement best.

## Nakau: the fourth name — and the oyakodon detour

Nakau belongs to the same group as Sukiya, and it sells gyudon too — but that is not why locals go. Nakau's signature is **oyakodon**, the chicken-and-egg rice bowl, plus proper udon noodles. Think of it as the beef bowl family member that covers the people who do not eat beef, or who simply prefer chicken and egg simmered in the same sweet dashi style.

**The flow.** Ticket machine first, like Matsuya: buy the meal ticket, hand it over, and your bowl arrives at the counter or your seat. If you are choosing between gyudon chains and someone in your group hesitates at beef, Nakau quietly solves the problem without leaving the price range.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Counter seats are a flow, not a lounge.** At lunch peak, eat, pay, and give the seat up. Reading a novel over an empty bowl while a line watches is the one real faux pas.
- **No tipping, anywhere in this article.** Staff cannot accept it; coins left behind will be treated as forgotten change. The exact price plus "gochisousama deshita" is the complete and correct thank-you.
- **Free toppings have a limit.** Pickled red ginger (beni shoga) sits on the counter for everyone. A spoonful on your bowl is the custom; building a pink mountain that empties the container is not.
- **At the ticket machine, decide before you stand there.** Step aside, read the board, then buy. The line's patience is real but not infinite.
- **Keep your space small.** Bags go under the counter or on your lap, not on the next seat — at peak hours that seat is someone's lunch.
- **Quiet is the default volume.** These are fast, calm rooms. Phone calls wait until outside.

## How locals actually choose

The honest answer is that most people do not re-decide every day. They pick the chain nearest their station or office, learn its flow once, and become regulars. Beyond habit, the split looks like this: Yoshinoya for the classic bowl eaten standing-adjacent in five minutes; Matsuya when you want soup and a slightly more complete meal for the same money; Sukiya for toppings, tables, families, and the middle of the night; Nakau when the craving is actually oyakodon or udon wearing a gyudon-chain price tag. Salarymen rotate by the day's appetite; students chase Sukiya's set deals; nobody is doing it wrong.
