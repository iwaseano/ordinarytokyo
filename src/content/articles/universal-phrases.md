**About the phrases in this guide:** every phrase below comes as a card. If pronouncing Japanese feels like too much, just open this page and show the card to the staff — you'll be understood. And if you do try saying it out loud, however it comes out, they'll be even happier that you tried.

One thing to know before the cards: Japanese people deeply respect the effort of trying to speak Japanese. Nobody is grading your Japanese — the attempt itself is what registers. Take the courage to say a phrase out loud, however it comes out, and you can feel the interaction change: people slow down, lean in, help you finish the sentence, and treat you more kindly than the situation requires. The cards on this page are the safety net, and they always work. Your voice, when you dare to use it, is the upgrade.

Every guide on this site carries its own phrase cards, tuned to its scene. But underneath them all sits a small shared core — the phrases that work in *every* shop, station, and restaurant in Japan, regardless of what is being sold. This page is that core, organized by the six moments you will actually meet them. Bookmark it before your first morning; by your third day you will be surprised how much of Japan these twenty cards unlock.

## Scene 1: Getting someone's attention

Japanese service runs on a polite fiction: staff are watching and ready, but the interaction starts when you signal. The signal is one word — **sumimasen** — said at normal volume toward the staff, or shown on the card. It means both "excuse me" and a light "sorry," and it is never wrong: calling a waiter, stopping a station attendant, apologizing for blocking an aisle. In restaurants with a call button on the table, the button replaces the word; everywhere else, this is the doorbell.

Where it happens: every restaurant guide on this site starts here — for example, [ordering at Saizeriya](/saizeriya-ordering/) or calling staff in the [drugstore guide](/drugstore-chains-compared/).

## Scene 2: Ordering by pointing

You do not need to read a menu to eat well in Japan. Point at the item — on the menu, in the display case, on the plastic model outside — and show **kore o kudasai** ("this one, please"). It works for dishes, products, and tickets alike. When the choice itself is the problem, hand the decision to the shop with **osusume o oshiete kudasai** ("please tell me your recommendation"): staff take the question as a compliment and will point at what the regulars order.

Where it happens: the [konbini starter list](/konbini-first-buys/) is built for pointing, and the recommendation question earns its keep in every chain comparison, from [gyudon](/gyudon-chains-compared/) to [kaiten sushi](/kaiten-sushi-chains-compared/).

## Scene 3: When you can't follow — or something is wrong

Fast Japanese will happen. The recovery kit is three cards deep: **mou ichido onegai shimasu** ("once more, please"), **yukkuri onegai shimasu** ("slowly, please"), and, when you want to check whether the whole conversation can switch languages, **eigo wa hanasemasu ka?** ("do you speak English?"). If the situation is bigger than a missed sentence — a gate that ate your ticket, a train going the wrong way, a lost bag — skip the details and show **komatte imasu** ("I'm in trouble"). It is the most efficient sentence in this collection: staff hear it and switch straight into problem-solving.

Where it happens: the [Suica guide](/suica-guide/) is this scene's home ground — nearly every gate problem ends at the staffed window with one of these cards.

## Scene 4: Paying and shopping

Registers in Japan ask questions (the asked-cards at the top of this page are the big four), and your side of the counter is mostly three phrases: **okaikei o onegai shimasu** ("the bill, please") at table-service restaurants, **kaado de onegai shimasu** ("by card, please") before you pay anywhere, and **fukuro o kudasai** ("a bag, please") when you want the bag that costs a few yen. Point cards deserve one warning: shop staff will ask about them constantly, and the answer for almost every visitor is the motte imasen card above — no form, no fuss, the transaction simply moves on.

Where it happens: the [konbini checkout guide](/konbini-checkout/) walks the whole register conversation in order, questions and answers both.

## Scene 5: Requests, permissions, and things you can't eat

Three small asks cover most of daily travel. **Shashin o totte mo ii desu ka?** ("may I take a photo?") before photographing inside any shop or restaurant — asking first is the custom, and the answer is usually a smiling nod. **Omizu o kudasai** ("water, please") at restaurants, where water is free. And the two food-safety cards: **karai desu ka?** ("is it spicy?") before ordering the unknown, and the "I can't eat X" pattern — **piinattsu wa taberaremasen** — with the food swapped in. For a real allergy, show that card *before* you order; Japanese kitchens take it seriously when they hear it in time.

Where it happens: the wasabi version of the can't-eat card lives in the [kaiten sushi guide](/kaiten-sushi-chains-compared/), and photo manners come up in the [sento and onsen guide](/sento-onsen-first-time/).

## Scene 6: Closing well

Japanese interactions end with small ceremonies, and visitors who join them leave a disproportionately good impression. **Daijoubu desu** smooths every "no, thank you" and "I'm fine." **Arigatou gozaimasu** is thanks in the present; **arigatou gozaimashita** is its past form, for help that is finished — the difference is small, and staff register it. Around food there are two bookends: **itadakimasu** when the food arrives, before the first bite, and **gochisousama deshita** — "thank you for the meal" — as you pay or leave, with **oishikatta desu** ("it was delicious") as the compliment that completes the set.

Where it happens: every food guide on this site ends with these — hear them in context in the [udon and soba guide](/udon-soba-chains-compared/) or the [curry guide](/japanese-curry-chains-compared/).
