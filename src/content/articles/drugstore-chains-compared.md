You will walk into a Japanese drugstore within your first two days in Tokyo — for a headache, for sunscreen, for the skincare everyone back home asked you to bring. And the first surprise is the scale of the place. A Japanese drugstore is not a pharmacy with a few shelves attached. It is a pharmacy, a cosmetics floor, and a small supermarket compressed into one bright, dense, slightly overwhelming room.

Three chains dominate Tokyo: Matsumoto Kiyoshi, Welcia, and Sundrug. They look similar from the sidewalk, but locals use them differently. This page compares what each chain is actually for.

## What is a Japanese drugstore?

The format grew out of a simple idea: the place you visit when something is slightly wrong should also sell everything you run out of at home. So one store carries prescription and over-the-counter medicine, cosmetics and skincare (often entire walls of it), shampoo and soap, laundry detergent, baby goods, snacks, drinks, and seasonal items from hand warmers to insect repellent. Many have a small food section priced below convenience stores.

The medicine corner follows its own rules, and two job titles matter:

- **Pharmacists (薬剤師)** wear a name badge that says so. Only they can dispense prescription medicine and sell the strongest category of over-the-counter drugs (Category 1), which require an explanation before purchase.
- **Registered sellers (登録販売者)** can sell the milder categories (Category 2 and 3) — most everyday cold, stomach, and pain medicines — but not Category 1.

Practical consequences for you: some medicines sit on open shelves you can pick up yourself, while others are kept behind the medicine counter or in locked cases, and staff will ask a few questions before handing them over. Those questions are a legal step, not suspicion.

## When you will actually use one

- **A symptom on the road** — headache, stomach trouble, a cold starting. This is the drugstore's original job, and staff are used to travelers describing symptoms by gesture and phone screen.
- **The souvenir and request run** — Japanese skincare, sunscreens, eye drops, and bath salts are standard requests from home, and drugstores are where locals buy them too.
- **Restocking a trip** — detergent pods for a coin laundry, spare masks, batteries, a cheap umbrella's worth of daily goods, all cheaper than at a convenience store.
- **Late at night** — many Welcia stores run long hours or 24 hours, and a drugstore with a pharmacy counter is the first stop for a problem that is not yet a hospital problem.

## Matsumoto Kiyoshi: the yellow sign, the cosmetics wall

Matsumoto Kiyoshi (everyone says "Matsukiyo") is the chain visitors meet first: the bright yellow sign is all over shopping districts, and the big stores are built for browsing. The cosmetics and skincare selection is the deepest of the three, with testers, ranking displays, and staff who expect questions. Prices are not always the lowest, but coupons in the chain's app and frequent point campaigns close much of the gap.

**What to know.** Tourist-area stores have dedicated tax-free counters and multilingual signage. If your list is mostly beauty products and gifts, start here and compare prices elsewhere only for big items.

## Welcia: the pharmacy that stays open

Welcia is the largest drugstore chain in Japan by sales, and its character is different: many stores have a proper prescription pharmacy counter, and a large share run late — some 24 hours. The floor leans toward daily goods and food more than glamour, which makes it the chain locals treat as infrastructure.

**What to know.** If you need a pharmacist at 10 p.m., Welcia is the most likely door to be open. The pharmacy counter and the general shelves are separate tills in some stores, so a prescription pickup and a shampoo run may be two queues.

## Sundrug: price first

Sundrug keeps its stores plainer and its pitch simple: the same national-brand medicine and daily goods, priced aggressively. You will see fewer testers and less theater than at Matsukiyo, and more locals buying a known list quickly.

**What to know.** For a fixed basket — a specific medicine, detergent, a known skincare staple — Sundrug is the comparison locals make first. Tax-free handling is available at many stores but the counters are less prominent, so ask at the register before you queue.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Answer the medicine questions properly.** For certain medicines, staff must confirm allergies, other medicines you take, and who the medicine is for. This is a legal safeguard, and vague answers can mean they cannot sell it to you. Quantities of some medicines are also limited per person — buying several boxes of the same strong medicine for gifts can be refused.
- **Tax-free changed in November 2026 — plan for the refund system.** Checked on 2026-11-26: you now pay the full price in store and claim the consumption tax back after customs inspection at departure. Keep tax-free goods unused and together with receipts until you leave Japan, bring the physical passport, and confirm the current procedure on the official tax-free site before a large purchase, because a new system means store procedures are still settling.
- **Be careful with photos inside.** Photographing your own purchased items is fine; photographing shelves, price tags in bulk, or other customers is not welcome in many stores. Ask staff, or take your notes outside.
- **No tipping.** Not at the register, not at the pharmacy counter. A thank-you is the complete form.
- **Testers are for testing, once.** Cosmetics testers are generous in Japan, but they are shared. Use the disposable applicators provided, and do not open sealed products — an opened item is a purchased item.

## How locals actually choose

Most people are loyal to whichever chain is on their street, and then they specialize: Matsukiyo for the fun of the cosmetics floor and the gift list, Welcia because the pharmacy counter saved them once at night and the daily goods are cheap, Sundrug because the same basket costs less and nobody needs a poster to buy detergent. Visitors can copy that split exactly — the chains are not interchangeable, they are three answers to three slightly different days.
