**About the phrases in this guide:** every phrase below comes as a card. If pronouncing Japanese feels like too much, just open this page and show the card to the staff — you'll be understood. And if you do try saying it out loud, however it comes out, they'll be even happier that you tried.

A Japanese convenience store is not a small supermarket. It is a kitchen, a drinks fridge, a stationery drawer and a small pharmacy, all fitted into one narrow room — and Tokyo locals use it that way, several times a week, for one meal or one missing thing at a time. This page is the map of those shelves: what each section is actually for, what locals buy from it, and what to skip.

Checkout itself is covered in the companion page, [How to Pay at a Japanese Convenience Store](/articles/konbini-checkout/). Read this page to choose, that page to pay.

## The shelves, in the order you meet them

**1. Onigiri — the first shelf, and the safest first buy.** Rice balls wrapped in seaweed, filled with salmon, tuna mayonnaise, pickled plum or seasonal fillings. Locals buy one or two as breakfast on the way to the station or as a late-night snack. The wrapper has a numbered pull-tab sequence — pull in order and the seaweed stays crisp. A deeper guide to flavours, prices by chain and the wrapper trick is planned as its own article.

**2. Sandwiches and bread — soft, sweet, and made that morning.** Egg sandwiches are the classic first try: soft white bread, a thick egg filling, no crust. Bakery shelves also hold melon bread, curry bread and seasonal rolls. These are made daily and sell out first, so the morning selection is the widest.

**3. Bento and noodles — a warm meal for about the price of a coffee elsewhere.** Chilled bento boxes, pasta and noodle cups can be heated in the store microwave — say yes when staff ask, and they hand it back hot. Check the microwave time printed on the lid; it is usually 500W for one to two minutes. A deeper guide to bento, noodles and the hot-snack case is planned as its own article.

**4. Drinks — the cold wall is half the store.** Bottled green tea, barley tea, canned coffee (hot and cold, in the same machine row in winter) and seasonal soft drinks. Unsweetened tea is the local default with a meal; sweetened drinks are clearly marked. Canned coffee from a convenience store is a normal workday habit, not a novelty.

**5. Snacks, sweets and dagashi — small, cheap, and easy to take home.** Potato chips and chocolate sit next to dagashi, the old-fashioned penny sweets sold in small packs for a few dozen yen. Dagashi is the section locals buy as souvenirs precisely because it is ordinary: the same sweets Japanese children have bought for decades. A deeper guide to snacks and dagashi is planned as its own article.

**6. Daily goods and disaster goods — the shelf visitors forget.** Pocket tissues, socks, umbrellas, phone chargers, basic medicine and stationery sit at the back. The same shelf also carries the practical side of Japanese daily life: face masks, hand towels, and small disaster-preparedness items such as portable lights and emergency food. A deeper guide to daily goods and disaster goods is planned as its own article.

## How to choose without reading Japanese

- **Price tags are on the shelf edge, in yen, under each item.** The number you see is the price you pay, including tax for takeaway.
- **Dates are printed on the pack, not the shelf.** Fresh food carries a sell-by date and time; check it on sandwiches and bento before you buy.
- **Seasonal labels mean limited.** A small flag or a different-coloured pack usually marks a seasonal or limited item. When it is gone, it is gone.
- **The hot-snack case is ordered, not taken.** Fried chicken and croquettes sit behind glass by the register. Point and say "kore o kudasai" — staff put it in a paper sleeve for you.

## Eat-in or takeaway: one question changes the price

If you eat inside the store, staff ask "tennai de omeshiagari desu ka?" — will you eat here? Eat-in food is taxed at 10%, takeaway at 8%, so the same onigiri costs slightly more if you eat it at the store's seats. Say "mochikaerimasu" for takeaway. If you do eat in, use the seats provided, and return trays and rubbish to the bins in that area when you finish.

## Manners and things not to do

The full checkout manners are on the companion checkout page — floor markers for the queue, waiting for the staff call, and paying before eating. The product shelves have their own short list:

**Do not:**
- **Open or start eating items before paying.** Everything is paid for first, including drinks on a hot day. There is no exception.
- **Open a pack on the shelf to check inside, or squeeze bread and onigiri to test them.** If you are unsure, ask staff with "osusume wa dore desu ka?"
- **Leave chilled food out of the fridge while you browse.** Take it when you are ready to pay, or put it back where you found it.
- **Block the aisle with luggage while you compare shelves.** Step to the side, or to the bagging area by the register, to sort your things.
- **Take photos of other customers or staff.** Photographing your own purchased items is fine; photographing people without asking is not.
- **Try to tip.** There is no tipping anywhere in Japan, and a convenience store register is the clearest case: staff cannot accept tips, and coins left on the counter will be chased after you as forgotten change.

## What locals actually do

Tokyo locals do not do a weekly shop at a convenience store. They stop in for one thing — an onigiri before the train, a coffee on the way back, a missing battery or an umbrella when it starts raining. The satisfaction is not luxury: it is that a ¥500 meal is warm, fresh and ready in the time it takes the light to change, and that the thing you forgot is always on the same shelf, at the same price, at 2 a.m. as at 2 p.m. Stock rotates through the day, shelves are refilled constantly, and seasonal items appear and disappear in weeks.
