Before a big exam, a job interview, or a match that matters, a lot of people in Japan eat the same thing the night before: katsudon. The reason is a pun — *katsu*, the fried pork cutlet, sounds exactly like *katsu*, "to win." Underneath the wordplay is a very practical meal: a freshly fried cutlet, simmered for a moment in sweet dashi broth with onion and egg, laid over a full bowl of rice. It is fast, it is filling, and it costs about the same as a coffee-shop lunch.

Three chains serve most of the katsudon eaten outside the home. They look similar from the street — bright signs, counter seats, a standard bowl front and center — but they order, price, and season things differently. Here is how they actually compare.

## What is katsudon?

Katsudon is a *donburi* — a bowl of rice with something hot served on top — and the something here is tonkatsu, a pork cutlet coated in panko breadcrumbs and deep-fried until crisp. The cutlet is sliced, then simmered briefly in a shallow pan with sliced onion and a sweet soy-and-dashi broth. Beaten egg is poured over it and cooked just until it sets, soft and steaming. The whole pan — cutlet, onion, egg, and sauce — is slid onto the rice in one piece and served with the lid on, so it arrives still bubbling slightly.

Two things surprise first-timers. First, the egg is not an omelette and the cutlet is not meant to stay fully crisp: the edges soften in the broth, and that contrast between the fried coating and the savory egg is the dish. Second, it is a complete meal in one bowl — protein, rice, and sauce together, usually with miso soup and pickles on the side.

The most common variation is **sauce katsudon**, which skips the egg entirely: the freshly fried cutlet is dipped in a sweet-savory sauce and laid over rice, often on a bed of shredded cabbage. It is the standard style in some regions, Fukui most famously, and all three chains below offer a version of it. Beyond that you will see seasonal and regional variations — miso-based, spicy, with cheese — but the egg-bound original is the reference point everything else is measured against. The *katsu* / "to win" pun from the introduction applies to all of them, which is why the bowl shows up before big days of every kind.

## When locals crave katsudon

Katsudon is not an everyday-default lunch in the way gyudon is; it is a bowl people choose on a particular kind of day.

- **The night before something that matters.** Exams, job interviews, matches, presentations. Part superstition, part strategy: it is the most filling, confidence-feeling dinner a student budget allows, and the pun does the rest.
- **When only something fried and hearty will do.** A cold day, a physically tiring day, a day that went badly. Katsudon is comfort food with weight — crisp fat, hot rice, sweet broth — and people reach for it the way others reach for a burger and fries, except it comes with soup.
- **When lunch has to carry you to dinner.** A standard bowl is a lot of food for the price. Workers and students pick it on days when the next meal is far away, because nothing at this price point keeps you full longer.
- **When it has simply been a while.** Because it is heavier than most fast lunches, katsudon works on rotation rather than routine — which is exactly why the chains' discount coupons and limited-time bowls exist: to give regulars a reason to make today the day.

## Katsuya: the reference standard

Katsuya exists for one thing: fried cutlets over rice. It is the largest katsudon chain in Japan, and for many people its bowl is simply what katsudon tastes like.

**How ordering works.** Stores vary — some have a ticket machine by the door, others take your order at the counter — but the menu logic is the same everywhere. The katsudon comes in grades named after pine, bamboo, and plum, which sound poetic and actually just mean cutlet size: plum is the standard 80-gram cutlet, bamboo is bigger. Start with plum; it is the bowl most of Japan eats.

**What to know.** Katsuya hands out a ¥100 discount coupon with almost every meal, valid on your next visit. Locals keep a small loop of these coupons going indefinitely — a quiet sign of how habitual this chain is. Portions run generous, and seasonal limited bowls (cheese, spicy, regional sauces) rotate through the menu.

## Matsunoya: the budget workhorse

Matsunoya belongs to the same group as the Matsuya gyudon chain, and it applies the same philosophy: keep the price as low as a fried-to-order cutlet allows.

**How ordering works.** Ticket machine at the entrance, almost always. Put in cash (or tap an IC card on newer machines), press the katsudon button, take the ticket, hand it to staff, and sit down. The machine flow is identical to a gyudon shop, so if you have used one of those, you already know this.

**What to know.** Matsunoya leans into set meals — cutlet, rice, miso soup, and a small salad — and some stores offer free rice and miso soup refills on sets, which makes it the chain of choice for very hungry students. The trade-off is atmosphere: it is built for speed and price, not lingering.

## Katsuan: the all-rounder

Katsuan is run by the Zensho group, the same company behind the Sukiya gyudon chain, and it is the most "family restaurant" of the three in spirit, while staying at fast-food prices.

**How ordering works.** Most stores take your order at your seat or the counter and you pay at the register at the end — no ticket machine to decode, which some first-timers find the most relaxing of the three. Menus have photos for everything.

**What to know.** The menu around the katsudon is wider: set meals, curry, and seasonal dishes sit alongside it, so a group where one person does not want katsudon can still eat together here. Stores are often a little more spacious, with more table seating than the counter-heavy layouts of the other two.

## Manners and what not to do

- **No tipping.** As everywhere in Japan, you pay the listed price and that is the end of it. Leaving coins on the tray will get them chased after you as forgotten change.
- **Fresh-fried means a short wait.** Unlike gyudon, which is served in a minute, a cutlet is fried after you order. Expect roughly five to ten minutes, a bit longer at peak. That wait is the product — a bowl that arrives instantly was not fried for you.
- **Do not camp at lunch.** Between about 11:30 and 13:30 these stores run at full speed with a line. Eat, enjoy it, and give up the seat. Outside peak hours, a slower pace bothers no one.
- **At the ticket machine, decide before you step up.** The line behind you is made of office workers on a thirty-minute lunch. Step aside, study the photo menu on the wall, then approach the machine when you know your button.
- **Sauce and toppings on the table are shared.** Use what you need, put bottles back where they were, and do not pour table sauce directly from the bottle onto food if a small dish is provided.

## How locals choose between them

The honest answer is that most people do not travel across town for one specific chain — katsudon is a "nearest good option" food. But habits do form. Katsuya is the default when someone says "katsudon" and the bowl students eat before exams. Matsunoya is where you go when the month is long and the wallet is thin, or when you plan to eat a genuinely large amount. Katsuan is the compromise choice for mixed groups and families, and the easiest first experience if ticket machines still make you nervous.

What satisfies people about all three is the same: a cutlet fried after you order, a bowl that arrives hot and complete for a few hundred yen, and a meal that takes fifteen minutes from door to door. The good-luck pun is a bonus; the engineering is the point.
