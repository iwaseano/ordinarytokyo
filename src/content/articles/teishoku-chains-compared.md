Fast food in Japan is not only burgers and beef bowls. On any weekday lunch hour, the longest lines of office workers often lead into a teishoku restaurant — a place serving set meals: one main dish, a bowl of rice, miso soup, pickles, and a small side or two, all arriving on one tray. It is the meal Japanese people mean when they say they ate "a proper meal" (chanto shita gohan). Nothing is fancy, and that is exactly the point: balanced, filling, reasonably priced, and the same quality every single day.

Three chains define the category, and they take three different approaches. **Ootoya** cooks like a careful home kitchen. **Yayoiken** runs on ticket machines and free rice refills. **Shinpachi Shokudo** does one thing — charcoal-grilled fish — and does it seriously. This page compares how each one works, what you will be asked, and which one fits which moment.

## What is teishoku?

If you have never met the word, start with the tray. A **teishoku** is a set meal with a fixed architecture: one **main dish** (grilled fish, fried pork cutlet, simmered vegetables, ginger pork — the protein is the headline), a bowl of **rice**, a bowl of **miso soup**, **pickles**, and one or two **small side dishes** (a little simmered vegetable, a small salad, a piece of tofu). Everything arrives at once, on one tray, for one fixed price. You do not build the meal; the meal is already composed.

The idea behind it is **ichiju-sansai** — "one soup, three dishes" — the traditional template of a Japanese home meal: rice as the base, one soup, and three dishes (a main and two smaller sides). Teishoku is that home template turned into a restaurant product. The point is not variety for its own sake; it is that the combination is balanced by design — protein, vegetables, something fermented, something warm and liquid — without the eater having to think about nutrition at all.

That is what separates teishoku from Japan's other everyday formats. A **donburi** (beef bowl, katsudon) is one bowl: toppings over rice, fast and filling, but essentially a single dish — there is no soup-and-sides structure around it. **Noodles** (ramen, soba, udon) are also a single-dish meal: complete in the bowl, often eaten in ten minutes standing or at a counter. A teishoku, by contrast, is a *spread* — several small things at once, eaten sitting down, in an order you choose. If donburi and noodles are Japan's fast food, teishoku is its cafeteria dinner: still quick and cheap, but recognizably a full meal. When a Japanese person feels they have been "eating badly," teishoku is the format that fixes it — which leads directly to when people choose it.

## When locals choose teishoku

Nobody eats teishoku by accident; it is the deliberate choice, and the moods behind it are remarkably consistent:

- **"I want a proper meal today."** After days of convenience-store lunches, skipped breakfasts, or heavy drinking nights, teishoku is the reset button. It is the meal people pick when they want to feel that they ate like a responsible adult — vegetables included, nothing deep-fried unless they chose it.
- **On a fish day.** Grilled fish is the dish Japanese home cooking has quietly given up on: small modern kitchens often lack a fish grill, and grilling fish fills a small apartment with smell. So "I want grilled fish" almost automatically means "I am eating out" — and a fish teishoku is where that craving lands.
- **At the weekday lunch break, sitting down.** Office workers rotate: noodles when there are ten minutes, a beef bowl when there are five, teishoku when there is a real lunch hour and they want to leave the restaurant feeling restored rather than just refueled.
- **When eating alone but not eating sadly.** Solo dining is completely normal at teishoku chains, and the tray format makes a one-person meal feel complete rather than makeshift. Students, workers, and elderly regulars all use it this way — it is everyday infrastructure, not an occasion.
- **Before or after something that matters.** Because it is balanced and not too heavy, teishoku is a common choice before an exam, an interview, or an afternoon that requires concentration — the meal that will not betray you at 3 p.m.

Keep this in mind as you read the comparison below: the three chains are not really competing on taste alone. They are competing on *which of these moods they serve best* — the quick reset, the home-style restoration, or the fish craving.

## Ootoya: the home kitchen, scaled up

Ootoya's identity is that each store cooks properly: fish is grilled to order, vegetables are cut in-store, and the menu reads like a month of Japanese home dinners — grilled fish with grated daikon, simmered pumpkin, chicken nanban with tartar sauce, pork with ginger. Portions of vegetables are noticeably larger than at rival chains, and many sets let you swap white rice for **gokokumai** (five-grain rice) at no extra charge.

**The flow.** Staff seat you and take your order at the table (some locations use a tablet). You will likely be asked about the rice swap (see the phrases above) and whether you want to upgrade the miso soup to tonjiru (pork soup) for a small fee. Food arrives on a tray, all at once. You pay at the register on the way out — keep the slip from your table.

**Good to know.** Ootoya is the slowest of the three in a good way: grilling to order takes time. It is a lunch you sit down for, not a lunch you squeeze in.

## Yayoiken: ticket first, rice forever

Yayoiken removes the hardest part for a visitor — talking to staff. At the entrance stands a **meal ticket machine**: choose your set (photos on the buttons, and many machines offer English), pay, take the ticket, sit down, and hand the ticket to staff or place it on the table. Your only job after that is eating.

**The famous refill machine.** At most locations, white rice refills are free and self-service. There is a rice machine in the store: bring your bowl, choose the amount on the button, and the machine serves it. It exists because a teishoku is calibrated so the side dishes are slightly too much for one bowl of rice — the second, smaller bowl is part of the design.

**The flow.** Ticket machine → sit → hand over ticket → eat → (refill rice if you like) → leave. Payment already happened at the machine, so there is no register stop on the way out.

## Shinpachi Shokudo: grilled fish, nothing else

Shinpachi Shokudo is a specialist: the menu is essentially a list of fish — mackerel (saba), Atka mackerel (hokke), salmon, yellowtail — each grilled over charcoal and served as a set with rice, miso soup, and pickles. Stores are compact, often counter-centered, and the smell of grilling fish reaches the street. That smell is the advertising.

**The flow.** Staff seat you (or point to a counter seat), you order from the menu at the table, and the fish arrives with the skin crisped in a way home kitchens rarely manage, because most Japanese homes no longer have a proper fish grill. That gap is the whole business model: this is the dish people cannot easily make for themselves anymore.

**Good to know.** Because the menu is narrow, a first order is easy: any grilled fish set is the right answer. If you do not know the fish names, the photos and the phrase in this page's phrase list will carry you.

## Manners and what not to do

- **No tipping, anywhere.** All three chains are pay-the-bill-exactly places. Trying to tip causes confusion, not gratitude.
- **Hold the rice bowl.** In Japan you lift the rice bowl (and the miso soup bowl) in one hand while eating with chopsticks in the other. Leaving the bowl on the tray and bending over it is called "dog eating" (inu-gui) and reads as sloppy.
- **Chopstick basics that actually matter.** Do not pass food from your chopsticks directly to someone else's chopsticks (hashi-watashi — it echoes a funeral ritual), do not stick chopsticks upright in rice (tate-bashi, also funeral imagery), and do not rub disposable chopsticks together to remove splinters — it quietly suggests the restaurant's chopsticks are cheap.
- **Rice refills have a social limit.** At Yayoiken the refill is free, and using it once is completely normal — the machine is there for it. Going back four or five times while ordering nothing else crosses from "hungry" into "taking advantage," and staff will notice.
- **Tray etiquette.** Everything arrives on one tray and that is how it stays. When you finish, leave the tray and dishes on the table as they are (these are not self-clearing restaurants) — staff handle the rest.
- **Quiet voices.** Teishoku shops at lunch are calm, efficient places. Normal conversation is fine; phone calls are taken outside.

## How locals actually choose

Ask a Tokyo office worker and the rotation is usually practical, not loyal. **Yayoiken** on the day you are short on time and money and want to leave genuinely full — the refill machine settles arguments. **Ootoya** on the day you feel you have eaten badly all week and want vegetables and a meal that tastes like someone cooked it — many people describe it as "the closest thing to a home dinner you can buy." **Shinpachi Shokudo** when the craving is specifically grilled fish, the dish that Japanese home cooking has quietly outsourced to restaurants as fish grills disappeared from small kitchens.

Solo diners are the norm at all three, at lunch especially. Nobody will find it strange if you eat alone, quickly, and in near silence. That, too, is part of the design.
