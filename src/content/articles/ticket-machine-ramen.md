**About the phrases in this guide:** every phrase below comes as a card. If pronouncing Japanese feels like too much, just open this page and show the card to the staff — you'll be understood. And if you do try saying it out loud, however it comes out, they'll be even happier that you tried.

At many ramen shops in Japan, there is no menu on the table and nobody comes to take your order. Instead, a vending machine by the door sells paper meal tickets, and that machine *is* the ordering system. It looks intimidating for about one minute — until you realize the whole process is fixed, visual, and designed to work even if you and the staff share no language at all.

This page follows one visit in order: the two kinds of machine you will meet, how to choose by photo and price, and the short questions staff ask after you hand over your ticket.

## The two types of machine

**The older button type: money goes in first.**
Rows of plastic buttons, each with a photo or a name and a price. On this type, you insert cash *before* choosing — the buttons that you can afford light up. Press the buttons for your bowl and any extras (extra noodles, an egg, a side of gyoza), and the machine prints one ticket per item and returns your change. Until money goes in, pressing buttons does nothing, which confuses many first-time visitors.

**The newer touch-panel type: choose first, pay last.**
A screen walks you through categories — ramen, toppings, sides, drinks — often with large photos and sometimes an English mode (look for "English" or a globe icon at the top). You build the order on screen and pay at the end. These are closer to a self-checkout and are becoming common in larger chains.

Either way, the result is the same: paper tickets that you hand to the staff. The tickets are your order and your receipt of payment in one.

## Choosing without reading Japanese

- **Photos and prices are the interface.** The signature bowl is usually the top-left or largest button. Prices cluster tightly — a regular bowl, a bowl with extra pork, a large size — so the price differences tell you what changes between buttons.
- **Decide before you reach the machine.** Most shops post the menu outside or above the machine. Use that, not the machine itself, as your thinking spot.
- **Cash readiness matters.** Many machines are cash-only and some reject ¥10,000 bills. Coins and ¥1,000 bills are the reliable combination; a convenience store ATM or register can break a large bill beforehand.

## After you hand over the ticket

Sit down, put your tickets on the counter where staff can take them, and expect one or two quick questions — most often about noodle firmness at tonkotsu shops, or optional garlic. These are not conversation; they are checkboxes. The phrases above cover the standard answers, and "futsuu" (regular) is always a safe choice.

Water is usually self-service from a dispenser or a pitcher on the counter. Your bowl arrives over the counter, often within a few minutes.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Decide away from the machine.** The line behind you is waiting to eat, not to watch you translate the whole menu. Step aside, look at the posted menu or photos, choose, then step up.
- **Keep your tickets until staff take them.** The ticket is proof you paid for that exact item. If you lose it, the shop has no way to know what you ordered — treat it like cash, because it is.
- **No tipping.** The price on the button is the whole price. Coins left on the counter will be treated as forgotten change, not a tip.
- **Be careful with photos inside.** Photographing your bowl is normal and welcome. Photographing other customers, the kitchen, or staff at work is not — keep the camera pointed at your own food, and skip photos entirely if a sign asks you to.
- **Eat at the shop's pace.** Ramen shops are built for turnover: eat while the noodles are good, and free the seat when you are done, especially if a line is waiting. Lingering over a phone with an empty bowl is the one habit locals notice.

## What locals actually enjoy about this system

The ticket machine is not a barrier — it is the reason a solo lunch in Tokyo can take twelve minutes door to door. No waiting to order, no waiting to pay, no bill to split, and the same bowl costs the same every time. Regulars have their button memorized and their firmness answer ready; the small ritual of ticket, counter, bowl, and "gochisousama deshita" on the way out is part of the pleasure. Once you have done it twice, you will understand the satisfaction: a system so standardized that it removes every decision except the one that matters — which bowl.
