On a cold evening in Tokyo, the warmest room in the neighborhood is not a restaurant or a bar. It is the public bath. Grandparents, office workers, students, and small children all end up in the same steaming water, and for many Tokyo residents this is not a special occasion at all — it is what you do when your own bathroom feels too small, too cold, or too ordinary.

Of all the "everyday Japanese life" experiences visitors can have, bathing is the one with the highest satisfaction and the most specific rules. The rules are not there to test you. They exist so that dozens of strangers can share one bath comfortably. Learn the sequence once, and a Tokyo sento becomes one of the easiest and most relaxing hours of your trip.

## Sento, onsen, and super sento: what is the difference?

The three words get mixed up constantly, so here is the clean version.

**Sento** is the neighborhood public bath. It uses ordinary heated water (sometimes with added minerals or herbal infusions), and its price is regulated — in Tokyo, every sento charges the same admission set by the metropolitan government. Sento exist because homes once had no baths; today they survive because people like them. Expect a high ceiling, a painted mural (often Mt. Fuji) on the bath wall, and a very local crowd.

**Onsen** means the water itself is natural hot spring water, drawn from a geothermal source and meeting legal standards for temperature and mineral content. A facility can be an onsen anywhere the spring exists — including in Tokyo, where a handful of sento and super sento pump up dark, mineral-rich spring water from deep underground.

**Super sento** is the large leisure version: many kinds of baths (open-air, jet baths, carbonated baths, sometimes a genuine onsen source), a sauna, a relaxation room, a restaurant, and manga corners. Prices are set by each facility, weekends cost more, and you can easily spend half a day. Think of it as a small spa theme park built for locals, not tourists.

For a first visit on a normal Tokyo day, a neighborhood sento is the purest version of the experience. A super sento is the comfortable version if you want variety and English-friendly scale.

## When locals go, and in what mood

Locals do not treat the bath as sightseeing. They go **after work on a day that drained them**, when the shoulders are stiff and the head is full. They go **on the coldest evenings of winter**, because a deep soak warms you for hours in a way a home shower never does. They go **with a parent or a grandchild**, because the bath is one of the few places generations still share without phones. And they go **before a day off**, when there is no reason to hurry home.

The mood is reset, not excitement. You arrive carrying the day; you leave slower, warmer, and slightly hungry. That is the entire product.

## How bathing works, step by step

1. **Arrive and take off your shoes.** At the entrance there are shoe lockers. Put your shoes in, take the key (often a wooden tag), and step up onto the raised floor. Everything from here is indoor flooring.
2. **Pay.** Traditional sento have a *bandai*, an attended counter between the men's and women's sides — pay the person there. Many modern sento and most super sento use a ticket machine instead: buy the admission ticket (plus sauna, if you want it) and hand it to the front desk. Rentals — towels, and at super sento the indoor wear — are sorted out here too.
3. **Enter the changing room.** Men's and women's entrances are marked by noren curtains: 男 (men, usually blue) and 女 (women, usually red). Undress completely, put everything including your large towel in a locker, and take only your small towel and toiletries into the bath area. Lock the locker and keep the key band on your wrist or ankle.
4. **Wash your whole body first.** Inside, take a stool and a wash bowl from the stack, and sit at a free washing station (a low shower and taps). Wash thoroughly — hair and body, everything — and rinse all the soap off. This is not a quick splash; it is the main rule of the entire culture, covered again below.
5. **Enter the tub clean.** Step in slowly — the water is hot, usually around 41–43°C. Sit, soak, and keep your towel and hair out of the water. Short soaks repeated are better than one long endurance session. Get out if you feel dizzy.
6. **Rinse, return, and dry off.** After your last soak, many people rinse lightly at the shower. Before you step back into the changing room, **wring out your small towel and wipe your body down at the bath-area entrance** so you do not drip across the dry floor.
7. **Dress, rest, and drink.** Changing rooms have mirrors, hair dryers, and usually a scale. In the lobby, the classic finish is a bottle of coffee milk or fruit milk from the glass-bottle fridge, drunk standing, one hand on a hip. Super sento extend this into full relaxation rooms and restaurants.

## Manners and what not to do

This is the section that decides whether your visit is comfortable for everyone, including you.

- **Wash before you soak — completely.** The tub water is shared and is not for cleaning. Going straight from the changing room to the tub is the one mistake that will genuinely bother people. Soap, shampoo, and rinse at the washing station first, every time.
- **Never put your towel in the tub.** The small towel is for washing and for carrying. In the water, it stays out: on the tub's edge, on a rock, or folded on top of your head — the classic look is practical, not a joke.
- **Keep your hair out of the water.** Tie up long hair. Hair in shared bath water is treated the same way as the towel: it does not go in.
- **No swimming, no diving, no splashing.** The tub is for sitting still. Children are welcome but are kept close and calm by their parents; copy that energy.
- **No photos, anywhere inside.** Cameras and phones do not belong in the changing room or bath area at all — not even of the empty room, not even of the mural. This is absolute, for privacy reasons, at every facility.
- **Dry off before the changing room.** Wipe down at the boundary so the dry floor stays dry. Wet footprints across the changing room are a small but real discourtesy.
- **Keep the washing station tidy.** Rinse the stool and bowl and put them back roughly as you found them. Do not leave a station running, and do not occupy one while you go back to soak.
- **Tattoos: check the facility's own rule.** Policies differ by facility — some welcome everyone, some refuse tattoos, some allow them covered. Confirm before you undress, at the desk or on the facility's site, and accept the answer as-is.
- **Modesty is normal, staring is not.** Everyone is naked and nobody is looking. Eyes up, conversation quiet, and the whole thing becomes unremarkable within two minutes — that is the design.

## Why locals are satisfied

A home bath in a Tokyo apartment is a small plastic unit you sit in alone. The sento is the opposite: a huge, tiled, echoing room, water deep enough to float your shoulders under, and a mural of Mt. Fuji that has watched the neighborhood soak for decades. The satisfaction is scale and warmth, plus something harder to export — the feeling of being a regular somewhere. Staff remember faces. The same people take the same spots. For the price of a coffee or two, you are not a customer being served; you are a neighbor sharing a bath.
