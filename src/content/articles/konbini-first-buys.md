The first time you walk into a Japanese convenience store, the problem is not finding something to buy. It is that everything looks worth buying: shelves of rice balls wrapped like small puzzles, sandwiches cut into perfect triangles, desserts in cups, fried chicken glowing in a glass case by the register. Japanese convenience stores — konbini — stock around 3,000 items in a space smaller than many hotel lobbies, and the selection turns over constantly.

This page is the starter list: one or two representative items from each category locals actually buy, so your first visit has a shape. Each category then gets its own detailed guide, linked as they are published.

## Start with the triangle: onigiri

Onigiri (rice balls) are the soul of the konbini. Salmon, tuna with mayonnaise, pickled plum, and kelp are the permanent lineup; seasonal and regional fillings rotate in and out. The wrapper is a small engineering trick — the seaweed is separated from the rice by a film so it stays crisp until you pull the tabs in the numbered order printed on the package (1, 2, 3). Pull them in order once, and you will never struggle again.

## The sandwich that ruins other sandwiches: tamago sando

The egg sandwich — soft white bread, no crusts, filled with finely chopped egg salad — is the item foreign visitors most often write home about. It costs very little and tastes like it should cost more. Every chain makes one, and comparing them is a legitimate local pastime. Sandwiches sit in the chilled case near the onigiri, next to wraps and burgers.

## Drinks: unsweetened is the default

The drinks wall is where visitors make their first mistake: in Japan, bottled teas are **unsweetened** unless the label says otherwise. Green tea, barley tea, and black oolong are everyday drinks, not health products. Coffee comes hot in small cans or bottles (look for the red "hot" markers on the shelf) and iced in cups you fill at the machine by the register. Each category — canned coffee, tea, juice, and the rotating cast of limited drinks — rewards a closer look.

## Hot snacks: order at the register

The glass case next to the register holds fried chicken, croquettes, steamed buns, and (in winter) oden, a simmered dish locals queue for. You do not serve yourself: you tell the staff what you want ("kore o kudasai" — this one, please). FamilyMart's Famichiki fried chicken is the most famous single item in this category.

## Dagashi: the 10-yen sweets shelf

Dagashi are traditional cheap sweets and snacks, priced for a child's allowance — some still cost around 10 to 30 yen. The Umaibo, a puffed corn stick in flavors from takoyaki to cheese, is the icon. You will find dagashi in a small section of the snacks aisle, and they make the lightest, cheapest, most conversation-starting souvenirs you can carry home.

## Beyond food: daily essentials and disaster goods

Two aisles surprise visitors most. The daily goods section stocks socks, underwear, shirts, umbrellas, phone chargers, and basic toiletries of genuinely usable quality — the store assumes you might need to rebuild a day from zero. And many stores carry a small disaster-preparedness corner: long-life food, water, emergency lights. Japan prepares for earthquakes the way other countries prepare for winter, and even that preparation is sold at the corner store.

## Made by a machine in the store: coffee and smoothies

Some of the best things in a Japanese convenience store are not on a shelf at all — you finish making them yourself, at a machine, after you pay.

**Register coffee** (all three big chains have it) works backwards from what most visitors expect. You pay at the register first and receive an empty cup in your size. Then you take the cup to the coffee machine yourself, set it under the spout, and press the button that matches your order — the buttons are usually split by hot/iced and size, with pictures. Lids, straws, sugar, and milk portions sit next to the machine. A cup costs roughly the same as a canned coffee, but it is freshly brewed, which is exactly why so many locals start their commute with one.

**Smoothies** (7-Eleven is the chain known for them) are a small ritual. In the freezer case you will find cups of frozen fruit and vegetables with a barcode. You buy the cup at the register like anything else, then peel the film, set the cup in the smoothie machine near the register, scan the barcode on the cup, and press start. The machine blends for about a minute. Nothing about it is obvious the first time — watch one local do it and the whole sequence makes sense.

Two things to get right with both machines: **pay first, always** (the cup is the product; the machine is just how you finish it), and **do not park yourself in front of the machine during the morning rush** — these sit in the busiest corner of the store, so make your drink, add your lid at the side counter, and move on.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Do not eat while walking.** Eating on the move (aruki-gui) is one of the most visible manners gaps in Japan. Locals eat standing still — outside the store by the wall, at a park bench, or back at the hotel. The food tastes the same; the impression is completely different.
- **Never open anything before paying.** Not a drink on a hot day, not a snack for a tired child. Everything is paid for first, every time.
- **The eat-in corner is not a lounge.** If the store has one, it is for eating food bought in that store. Do not bring in food from elsewhere, spread out luggage, charge every device, or settle in to work. Eat, tidy your tray and wrappers into the store's sorted bins, and free the seat.
- **Tell the staff if you are eating in.** The tax rate differs (10% eat-in vs 8% takeout for food), and staff will ask. Answering honestly is part of the system working.
- **Do not tip** (see the checkout guide) and **do not leave trash on the eat-in table** — bins are right there, sorted by type.

## Why locals are satisfied with a corner store

None of these items is luxurious. The satisfaction is reliability: the onigiri at 7 a.m. in Tokyo tastes the same as the one at midnight, the egg sandwich is always soft, the seasonal dessert you loved will be gone in three weeks and replaced by another one. Tokyo locals do not think of konbini as shops of last resort — they are small, trusted kitchens and pantries that happen to also sell socks and light bulbs. Your first three items will show you why.
