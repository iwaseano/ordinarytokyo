Japanese curry is not a special-occasion food. It is what families cook on weeknights, what school cafeterias serve, and what office workers eat standing between meetings. The roux is thick and glossy, the spice is gentle unless you ask otherwise, and the plate always comes with rice and a small pile of bright red pickles. Because the dish started as home cooking, the chains that serve it compete on everyday things — price, speed, and how precisely you can tune the plate to your mood — rather than on ceremony.

This page compares the three curry chains a first-time visitor is most likely to walk into in Tokyo: **CoCo Ichibanya**, the customisation specialist; **Go Go Curry**, the Kanazawa-style heavyweight; and **Matsuya**, the beef-bowl chain where curry is the quiet second act.

## What is Japanese curry?

Japanese curry is a thick, glossy sauce served over a mound of white rice, usually with meat and soft-cooked vegetables such as potato, carrot, and onion melted into it. The sauce is made from a roux — flour and fat cooked with curry spices — so it coats the rice instead of soaking into it, and each spoonful holds together like a stew. Most plates arrive half rice, half sauce, with a small pile of fukujinzuke, the sweet red pickles that reset your mouth between bites.

If you know Indian curry, expect something quite different: Japanese curry is far less about heat or distinct whole spices, and more about a rounded, savoury-sweet depth. The standard version is mild enough for schoolchildren, and sweetness from onion, fruit, or honey is a feature, not an accident. If you know Thai curry, the difference is texture — no coconut milk, no broth-like liquid, nothing soupy. Japanese curry is closer to a beef stew that happens to be curry-flavoured, eaten with a spoon over rice rather than with bread or naan.

Spiciness exists on a range rather than as a fixed identity. The same dish can be *amakuchi* (sweet and mild, made with children in mind), a gentle regular level, or aggressively hot at specialty chains where you choose the heat by number. Heat is a dial each diner sets, not a test the dish sets for you.

The dish arrived in Japan in the Meiji era, adapted from the curry served in the British navy, and spread through the Japanese navy, school lunches, and home kitchens. Boxed roux blocks made it one of the easiest full meals a household could cook, and it settled in as a family staple — the dinner parents make in a big pot, better on the second day, and served at home with whatever meat and vegetables are on hand. Curry restaurants grew out of that familiarity: they sell a home dish, made consistent and fast, to people who already have a personal standard for how it should taste.

## When locals crave it

Japanese curry is chosen by mood as much as by hunger. It is the classic "I want something hearty today" food — warm, filling, and guaranteed to leave you full — so it appears on workdays when lunch has to carry a long afternoon, and on evenings when a thin or delicate meal would feel like a disappointment.

It is also the taste of home. Because so many people grew up on the boxed-roux version, a chain curry can satisfy a specific homesickness: students living alone, workers far from family, and anyone having the kind of day where food from childhood is the point. The toppings people add — cheese, a soft egg, fried chicken — are often recreations of what their household did, not just flavour upgrades.

A third mood is indecision. Curry accepts almost any topping and any appetite, so it becomes the compromise meal for groups whose tastes differ, and the safe answer on days when nothing else sounds right. When a local says "curry feels right today," it usually means one of these three things: something solid, something familiar, or something that ends the discussion.

## CoCo Ichibanya: the customisation specialist

CoCo Ichibanya (everyone just says "CoCo Ichi") treats curry like a build-your-own system. You choose four things, in this order:

1. **The base curry and toppings.** Start with a plain pork or beef curry, then add toppings from a long photo menu — cheese, spinach, soft-cooked egg, fried chicken, sausages, vegetables, seafood. Each topping has its own price, so the plate can stay simple or climb.
2. **The rice amount, by weight.** The standard is about 300 grams, which surprises most visitors. Smaller and larger amounts change the price slightly.
3. **The spice level, by number.** This is the question staff will ask: "karasa wa dou saremasu ka?" Level 0 (regular) is mild-medium. Numbers climb from there, and the top levels are genuinely punishing. Going the other way, *amakuchi* is a sweet, mild version made to be safe for children and spice beginners.
4. **Extras.** Fukujinzuke pickles are on the table in a shared pot at most stores.

The satisfaction here is control. Locals have personal "usual" combinations — a regular order they have refined over years — and the chain is built to remember that feeling even if you are inventing yours on the spot.

## Go Go Curry: Kanazawa style, no decisions wasted

Go Go Curry serves Kanazawa curry, a regional style from the city of Kanazawa: a very thick, dark, almost black sauce, served in a stainless-steel dish with shredded cabbage on the side and, on the signature plate, a pork cutlet covered in sauce. The flavour is rich and concentrated rather than fiery.

The flow is the opposite of CoCo Ichi. You order at the counter or a ticket machine, choose a size, and the plate arrives quickly. There is no spice ladder to climb and no topping spreadsheet — the customisation is deliberately small, which is the point. The cabbage is not decoration: its freshness cuts through the heaviness of the sauce, and eating them together is the intended balance.

Portions are serious. A medium is a full lunch for most people, and the larger sizes are aimed at students and workers who plan to skip dinner. If you finish a large and feel fine, you have understood something about Kanazawa.

## Matsuya: the beef-bowl chain's second act

Matsuya is famous first for beef bowls and set meals, and its curry lives in that ecosystem: fast, cheap, and available at hours when almost nothing else is open. You buy a meal ticket from the machine at the entrance — many Matsuya stores are ticket-first — hand it over or have it scanned, and the food follows within minutes.

The curry itself is a straightforward, home-style plate, often ordered as a beef curry or paired with the chain's other set meals. There is little customisation, and that is the trade: Matsuya is where curry costs the least of these three and asks the least of you. For locals, it is less "let's go for curry" and more "it's late, I'm hungry, Matsuya is open" — which, in Tokyo daily life, is one of the highest compliments a chain can earn.

## Manners and what not to do

- **No tipping, anywhere.** All three chains run on exact prices. Coins left behind will be treated as forgotten change.
- **Go easy on the free pickles.** Fukujinzuke (and the cabbage and table seasonings) are shared, free, and refilled by the store — take what you will actually eat, use the serving spoon or tongs provided, and do not heap a mountain onto the plate as a free side dish.
- **Do not linger at lunch peak.** These are fast-turnover restaurants. At midday, office workers are queueing behind you; finishing, clearing your tray if the store is self-service, and leaving promptly is the local rhythm. Off-peak, nobody will rush you.
- **Keep your tray and table as you found them.** Return trays to the return counter where one exists, and wipe up your own spills with the table cloth or a napkin.
- **Photograph your own food, not the room.** A quick photo of your plate is normal. Filming staff, other customers, or the whole dining room is not.

## How locals actually choose

The three chains are not really rivals in most people's heads — they are tools for different days. CoCo Ichi is the "I know exactly what I want" day, and the default for eating with someone whose spice tolerance differs from yours, because each plate is tuned separately. Go Go Curry is the "I need a serious lunch" day, chosen when the afternoon is long. Matsuya is the gravity option: nearest, cheapest, open, and good enough that "good enough" becomes a genuine preference over years of late shifts and early trains.

If you only have time for one on a first trip, start with CoCo Ichi for the customisation experience — then try to notice, over the rest of your trip, how often a Matsuya sign is simply *there* when you need it.
