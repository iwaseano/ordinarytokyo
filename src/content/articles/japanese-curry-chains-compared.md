Japanese curry is not a special-occasion food. It is what families cook on weeknights, what school cafeterias serve, and what office workers eat standing between meetings. The roux is thick and glossy, the spice is gentle unless you ask otherwise, and the plate always comes with rice and a small pile of bright red pickles. Because the dish started as home cooking, the chains that serve it compete on everyday things — price, speed, and how precisely you can tune the plate to your mood — rather than on ceremony.

This page compares four curry chains a first-time visitor is most likely to walk into in Tokyo: **CoCo Ichibanya**, the customisation specialist; **Go Go Curry**, the Kanazawa-style heavyweight; **My Curry Shokudo**, the curry specialty shop run by the Matsuya group; and **Hinoya Curry**, the old-school champion from Kanda, Tokyo's curry battleground.

## What is Japanese curry?

Japanese curry is a thick, glossy sauce served over a mound of white rice, usually with meat and soft-cooked vegetables such as potato, carrot, and onion melted into it. The sauce is made from a roux — flour and fat cooked with curry spices — so it coats the rice instead of soaking into it, and each spoonful holds together like a stew. Most plates arrive half rice, half sauce, with a small pile of fukujinzuke, the sweet red pickles that reset your mouth between bites.

If you know Indian curry, expect something quite different: Japanese curry is far less about heat or distinct whole spices, and more about a rounded, savoury-sweet depth. The standard version is mild enough for schoolchildren, and sweetness from onion, fruit, or honey is a feature, not an accident. If you know Thai curry, the difference is texture — no coconut milk, no broth-like liquid, nothing soupy. Japanese curry is closer to a beef stew that happens to be curry-flavoured, eaten with a spoon over rice rather than with bread or naan.

**If you know curry from Britain, the story doubles back on itself.** Curry is famous in Britain too — but British curry is a different branch of the family. Japan's curry arrived on British ships: the Royal Navy's roux-thickened shipboard curry was adopted by the Imperial Japanese Navy in the Meiji era and then walked into home kitchens. Britain's own curry culture, meanwhile, was rebuilt by South Asian cooks into the curry-house tradition — chicken tikka masala, balti, vindaloo — thinner sauces, distinct whole spices, naan for scooping, and heat as a headline feature. Japan took the naval version and made it thicker, sweeter, and milder until it became a family stew. So a British visitor tasting Japanese curry is meeting a distant cousin of a dish Britain exported — and the joke completes itself in the last decade, with London's katsu curry boom importing the Japanese descendant back to Britain as a novelty.

Spiciness exists on a range rather than as a fixed identity. The same dish can be *amakuchi* (sweet and mild, made with children in mind), a gentle regular level, or aggressively hot at specialty chains where you choose the heat by number. Heat is a dial each diner sets, not a test the dish sets for you.

**The roux is where the chains part ways.** All four sauces in this article are "Japanese curry," but they sit at opposite ends of a texture spectrum, and confusing them is the easiest mistake a first-timer can make. CoCo Ichibanya's sauce is smooth and pourable — it flows over the rice and coats it like a sauce. Go Go Curry's is the opposite extreme: so thick it barely qualifies as a liquid, a dense paste that clings to the rice and has to be scooped, not poured. My Curry Shokudo pours a middle-weight sauce in several styles, and Hinoya's roux plays a timing trick — sweet on the first bite, with the heat arriving afterwards. Each chain's section below describes its own roux, because the roux *is* the chain.

The dish arrived in Japan in the Meiji era, adapted from the curry served in the British navy, and spread through the Japanese navy, school lunches, and home kitchens. Boxed roux blocks made it one of the easiest full meals a household could cook, and it settled in as a family staple — the dinner parents make in a big pot, better on the second day, and served at home with whatever meat and vegetables are on hand. Curry restaurants grew out of that familiarity: they sell a home dish, made consistent and fast, to people who already have a personal standard for how it should taste.

## When locals crave it

Japanese curry is chosen by mood as much as by hunger. It is the classic "I want something hearty today" food — warm, filling, and guaranteed to leave you full — so it appears on workdays when lunch has to carry a long afternoon, and on evenings when a thin or delicate meal would feel like a disappointment.

It is also the taste of home. Because so many people grew up on the boxed-roux version, a chain curry can satisfy a specific homesickness: students living alone, workers far from family, and anyone having the kind of day where food from childhood is the point. The toppings people add — cheese, a soft egg, fried chicken — are often recreations of what their household did, not just flavour upgrades.

A third mood is indecision. Curry accepts almost any topping and any appetite, so it becomes the compromise meal for groups whose tastes differ, and the safe answer on days when nothing else sounds right. When a local says "curry feels right today," it usually means one of these three things: something solid, something familiar, or something that ends the discussion.

## CoCo Ichibanya: the customisation specialist

CoCo Ichibanya (everyone just says "CoCo Ichi") treats curry like a build-your-own system. You choose four things, in this order:

1. **The base curry and toppings.** Start with a plain pork or beef curry, then add toppings from a long photo menu — cheese, spinach, soft-cooked egg, fried chicken, sausages, vegetables, seafood. Each topping has its own price, so the plate can stay simple or climb.
2. **The rice amount, by weight.** The standard is about 300 grams, which surprises most visitors. Smaller and larger amounts change the price slightly.
3. **The spice level, by number.** This is the question staff will ask: "karasa wa dou saremasu ka?" — and it deserves respect, because CoCo Ichi's ladder is not a gentle slope. The standard sauce itself is smooth and pourable, a flowing roux that coats the rice — but the heat inside it climbs fast. "Regular" is already a proper medium, about the heat of a Japanese store-bought medium curry. **Level 1 is roughly double that**, past a typical store-bought "hot." Level 5 is unambiguously fiery food. Above that, the menu itself starts gating you: levels 6–10 are served to people who have finished a level 5, and the top rungs — the ladder now runs to 15 and even 20 — require finishing the level below first. Read that again: the chain will not sell you its hottest curry until you have proven, plate by plate, that you can finish the previous one. That is not marketing. It is a safety system.
   
   So the advice for a first visit is plain: **do not start hot.** Order regular, or level 1 if you know you like heat. Every table has a free shaker of *tobikara* spice — shake it over your plate and the heat rises as you eat, in small steps you control. Heat added can be managed; heat ordered cannot be taken back out of the sauce. Going the other way, *amakuchi* is a sweet, mild version made to be safe for children and spice beginners, and cheese or a soft egg can rescue a plate that turned out hotter than planned.
4. **Extras.** Fukujinzuke pickles are on the table in a shared pot at most stores.

The satisfaction here is control. Locals have personal "usual" combinations — a regular order they have refined over years — and the chain is built to remember that feeling even if you are inventing yours on the spot.

**Takeout is a first-class citizen here.** CoCo Ichi is one of the easiest curry chains to take home: takeout is available across the chain, you can order ahead by phone or the official app and skip the wait entirely, and the smooth, pourable sauce travels well — the plate survives the trip home intact. Expect a small takeout surcharge (¥54 including tax on the standard pork curry), and delivery is offered from many stores. For locals, "CoCo Ichi at home" is as normal as eating in: it is the number people call when the family wants curry night without anyone cooking.

## Go Go Curry: Kanazawa style, no decisions wasted

Go Go Curry serves Kanazawa curry, a regional style from the city of Kanazawa — and its roux is the exact opposite of CoCo Ichi's. Where CoCo's sauce pours, Go Go's barely moves: a very thick, dark, almost black roux that sits **between solid and liquid**, dense enough to cling to every grain of rice it touches and stay where the spoon puts it. The chain says the sauce is simmered through 55 steps over 5 hours, then rested for 55 hours to mature. It arrives in a stainless-steel dish, poured over the rice and over the pork cutlet on the signature plate, with shredded cabbage on the side and a drizzle of sauce on the cutlet. You eat it with a fork or a spork, Kanazawa style — a spoon would bend under the roux's authority. The flavour is rich and concentrated rather than fiery.

The flow is the opposite of CoCo Ichi too. You order at the counter or a ticket machine, choose a size, and the plate arrives quickly. There is no spice ladder to climb and no topping spreadsheet — the customisation is deliberately small, which is the point. The cabbage is not decoration: its freshness cuts through the heaviness of the roux, eating them together is the intended balance, and refills of it are free at many stores.

Portions are serious. A medium is a full lunch for most people, and the larger sizes are aimed at students and workers who plan to skip dinner. If you finish a large and feel fine, you have understood something about Kanazawa.

And because this is Kanazawa curry, **the main event is katsu curry**. The Roast Katsu Curry is the chain's runaway No. 1 plate; the plain curry exists mostly as a base camp for toppings. If you order one thing here, that is the order.

**The challenge plate.** Go Go Curry also runs a formal food challenge: the **Major Curry World Champion Class** — about 2.5 kilograms of curry, rice, cutlet, and toppings on one plate, limited to five servings a day per store. Finish the whole thing and your photo joins the champions on the chain's official website, which is either immortality or evidence, depending on how the attempt felt. Two structural warnings. The challenge is **eat-in only, and the leftovers cannot be taken home** — the plate is finished at that table or not at all. And 2.5 kg is not a large lunch; it is several lunches in a trench coat. Do not attempt it on a whim, on a dare, or on the theory that curry is mostly liquid — at Go Go, as established above, it is not. Most people never attempt it, and the regulars respect that decision.

## My Curry Shokudo: the curry specialist inside the beef-bowl empire

Matsuya — the beef-bowl chain — also runs a curry specialty brand: **My Curry Shokudo** ("My Curry Diner"), operated by Matsuya Foods. Most of its locations are not standalone shops but a curry counter built inside a Matsuya or a Matsunoya (the group's pork-cutlet chain), sharing the entrance and the ticket machine: you buy your curry ticket from the same machine that sells beef bowls, and the curry side of the kitchen makes your plate. A smaller number of standalone My Curry Shokudo stores exist too.

Because it is a specialist, the menu is all curry, in a way Matsuya's own never is: a plain **Original Curry for ¥550**, a roast katsu curry (¥780), omelette curries, and a choice of sauces — the house original, a richer European-style beef sauce, and a spicy one. The roux sits between the two extremes of this article: a proper sauce with body, thicker than CoCo Ichi's, far from Go Go's paste. There is no numbered spice ladder; you choose a sauce instead. What My Curry Shokudo inherits from its parent is price discipline and hours — this is the cheapest curry in the article, served with beef-bowl-chain reliability.

And a note for the days when there is no My Curry Shokudo nearby: **Matsuya itself also serves curry** — a beef curry sits on the same ticket machine as the beef bowls at ordinary Matsuya stores. It is the plain, home-style version, and for locals it plays the gravity role: less "let's go for curry" than "it's late, I'm hungry, Matsuya is open" — which, in Tokyo daily life, is one of the highest compliments a chain can earn.

## Hinoya Curry: sweet first, then the heat chases you

Hinoya Curry is the old soul of this comparison. It comes from Kanda, the central-Tokyo district that takes curry seriously enough to run an annual Kanda Curry Grand Prix — which Hinoya won, becoming the event's first hall-of-fame inductee. The shops are small, counter-led, and unpretentious; many use a ticket machine at the door.

The roux is the signature, and it works by timing. The first bite is **sweet** — onions cooked down until they melt into the sauce — and then, a few seconds later, **the heat arrives and chases it**, leaving a lingering warmth instead of a blast. It is the flavour profile of old Tokyo curry shops, the "nostalgic" taste Japanese diners describe with the word *natsukashii*. The standard order is the **Signature Katsu Curry**, the cutlet breaded and fried in-store; the cut is made in a distinctive grid pattern so each piece is a full mouthful. Portions run generous — a regular serving is about 300 grams of rice, large rice is free at many stores, and a seven-tenths portion (*shichibu-mori*) exists for smaller appetites.

There is no customisation system to learn and no spice dial to set: one roux, honestly made, at one fixed character. After CoCo Ichi's spreadsheet and Go Go's density, Hinoya is the argument that a curry shop needs exactly one sauce — as long as it is this one.

## Manners and what not to do

- **No tipping, anywhere.** All four chains run on exact prices. Coins left behind will be treated as forgotten change.
- **Go easy on the free pickles.** Fukujinzuke (and the cabbage and table seasonings) are shared, free, and refilled by the store — take what you will actually eat, use the serving spoon or tongs provided, and do not heap a mountain onto the plate as a free side dish.
- **Do not linger at lunch peak.** These are fast-turnover restaurants. At midday, office workers are queueing behind you; finishing, clearing your tray if the store is self-service, and leaving promptly is the local rhythm. Off-peak, nobody will rush you.
- **Leave the tray on the table at these four chains.** CoCo Ichi, Go Go, My Curry Shokudo, and Hinoya are staff-clearing restaurants — there is no return counter, and wandering off with your tray in search of one only confuses everyone. What is expected of you is smaller: wipe up your own spills with the napkin or table cloth, leave the dishes grouped neatly, and push your chair in. The exception is a food-court location — there the food court's rules win, and the tray goes back to the shared return stand.
- **Photograph your own food, not the room.** A quick photo of your plate is normal. Filming staff, other customers, or the whole dining room is not.

## How locals actually choose

The four chains are not really rivals in most people's heads — they are tools for different days. CoCo Ichi is the "I know exactly what I want" day, and the default for eating with someone whose spice tolerance differs from yours, because each plate is tuned separately. Go Go Curry is the "I need a serious lunch" day, chosen when the afternoon is long. My Curry Shokudo is the value play — a specialist's menu at beef-bowl prices, often without leaving the Matsuya you were already passing. Hinoya is the mood choice: the day you want the old Tokyo taste, sweet first and warm after, from a shop that has won arguments about it in Kanda.

If you only have time for one on a first trip, start with CoCo Ichi for the customisation experience — then try to notice, over the rest of your trip, how often a Matsuya sign is simply *there* when you need it, and how many of them now hide a curry counter inside.
