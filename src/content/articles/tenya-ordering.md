Tempura has an image problem abroad: it reads as a special-occasion food, something ordered piece by piece at a quiet counter while a chef watches you eat it. In Tokyo daily life, tempura is also a fast lunch in a bowl — eaten in fifteen minutes at a chain store between trains. **Tenya** is the chain that made that happen. It is a tendon specialist: nothing but tempura rice bowls, tempura sets, and tempura with soba, fried to order and priced like a beef bowl.

The numbers tell the story. The cheapest tendon costs **630 yen**, the classic original bowl **730 yen**, and every bowl arrives with miso soup included and the tempura still crackling from the fryer. ## First, what is tendon?

*Donburi* is the family all of these bowls belong to, and the word means two things at once: the deep, wide bowl itself, and the dish served in it — steamed white rice with something cooked placed on top. The design is deliberate: the topping is almost always saucy, and the sauce is meant to soak into the top layer of rice, so every bite carries both. Gyudon (beef and onion), katsudon (pork cutlet with egg), oyakodon (chicken and egg) are the siblings; **tendon** is the fried one.

Even the name is a shortcut. Japanese shortens long words as a reflex, and *tendon* is **tempura-don** with the middle squeezed out — *tempura* plus *don*, the clipped form of donburi. Once you hear it, the whole family reads the same way: gyudon is beef-don, katsudon is cutlet-don. The dish is exactly what its compressed name says: tempura, served as a donburi.

Tempura itself is seafood and vegetables coated in a light, cold batter and deep-fried briefly, so the coating turns crisp and pale gold while the inside steams. Done well it is not greasy — the batter is a thin shell, not a crust. **Tendon** builds a rice bowl out of it: assorted tempura arranged over rice and finished with *tare*, a sweet, dark soy-based sauce poured over the top. The sauce softens the batter where it touches and seasons the rice below — crisp and soaked in the same bite, which is exactly the donburi idea.

**When locals crave it.** Gyudon and katsudon are simmered and soft; tendon is the donburi with crunch, so people rotate to it for texture when the plain rice bowls start to feel repetitive. Shrimp tempura still feels slightly festive, which makes tendon the "a little better than usual" lunch that stays within a coin or two of the cheapest options. And it scratches the fried-food itch at lunchtime without committing to a full tonkatsu set — one bowl, miso soup, done. If your mood is "something crisp, something a bit celebratory, still fast and cheap," you are in a tendon mood.

## How ordering works, step by step

Tenya has three ordering patterns across its stores, and the one in front of you is the right one. Most stores work like this:

1. **Take a seat.** Counter seats and tables, usually free seating — sit where you like. A menu book is on the table.
2. **Decide from the menu book.** The tendon lineup is on the first pages, with photos. Decide your bowl, your rice size, and your sauce amount now — you will be asked.
3. **Order with the staff.** Say "sumimasen" and order, or use the phrase cards on this page. At some newer stores a tablet on the table takes the order instead; the paper menu book is still there if the tablet defeats you.
4. **Wait a few minutes.** Your tempura is fried to order. A short wait is the system working, not a delay — this is the entire point of the chain.
5. **Eat.** The bowl arrives with miso soup. Tempura goes from crisp to soaked by design, so eat promptly (one photo, then eat).
6. **Pay at the register on your way out.** Nobody brings a bill to the table. Cards and IC cards are accepted at most stores.
7. **Leave the bowls on the table.** Tenya staff clear the tables — there is no tray to return and no return shelf to find.

**The ticket-machine variant.** Some stores (often busy station locations) put a ticket machine by the door: decide from the board, buy the ticket for your bowl, hand it to the staff, then sit. You have already paid, so step 6 disappears. **The takeout variant.** Many stores have a separate takeout counter or window; the bento versions of the bowls cost exactly the same as eating in, and mobile order pickup exists.

## The system to understand: one lineup, three ways to eat it

Tenya's menu looks long, but it is one idea repeated. There are **seven tempura lineups** — the combinations of seafood and vegetables the kitchen fries. Each lineup can be eaten three ways:

- **As a tendon** — the tempura laid over rice in a bowl, sauce poured over, miso soup included. The default, and the cheapest form.
- **With Yabu soba** — the tempura on a plate beside a serving of Yabu soba, Tokyo's sharp, classic soba. A small soba or a full-size one; hot or cold; soba swappable for udon. This is the traditional tempura pairing, priced a few hundred yen above the bowl.
- **As a teishoku set meal** — the tempura on a plate with rice, miso soup, a small side dish, and pickles. Eaten piece by piece with salt or dipping sauce, this is the slow, tempura-restaurant form of the same ingredients.

Around that frame sit the adjustments: rice size (small −¥50, large +¥100, extra-large +¥170; on teishoku sets the large rice is free), sauce amount (extra or light, free, decided when you order), the miso-soup-to-tonjiru swap (+¥250), and single extra pieces of tempura from ¥100 up. Learn the frame once and the whole menu book is readable.

## The bowls locals actually order

Because this page is about one chain, the menu gets the spotlight. All seven tendon, in the order a first visit should meet them. Every price includes miso soup.

**Ganso Tenya no Tendon — the original (730 yen).** "Ganso" means the originator, and this is the canonical bowl the chain was built on: a shrimp, an opened kisu (a delicate white fish), squid, eggplant, and green beans. If you want to know what a standard tendon is supposed to taste like, this is the reference bowl — balanced between seafood and vegetables, nothing oversized, the sauce doing quiet work underneath.

**All-Star Tendon — the signature (860 yen).** The bowl Tenya itself calls its signature, and the one on the posters: shrimp, chicken tempura, squid, maitake mushroom, lotus root, and eggplant — six pieces, the full bench of popular items in one bowl. The chain has renewed this bowl nine times over the years, adjusting the lineup the way a sports team adjusts a roster, and it is the default order for regulars who are not watching the budget line. Order this when you want the chain's best argument for itself.

**Tenya no Jo Tendon — the shrimp upgrade (760 yen).** "Jo" means premium-grade, and the upgrade is focused: **two shrimp**, with lotus root, pumpkin, and green beans. This is the bowl for people whose favorite part of any tendon is the shrimp and who find one shrimp a tease. For thirty yen above the original, it is the quiet value pick of the menu.

**Tenya no Tokujo Tendon — the celebration bowl (1,160 yen).** "Tokujo" is the top grade: a **whole anago** (sea eel), two shrimp, lotus root, and green beans. The anago alone runs the length of the bowl. This is tendon as a small event — the one locals order on a good day, and the closest the chain comes to a tempura restaurant's counter price, at roughly half of it.

**Tori to Maitake no Tendon — the cheapest bowl (630 yen).** Chicken tempura and maitake mushroom lead, with eggplant, lotus root, and green beans. No seafood at all, which is how the price lands at the bottom of the menu. Chicken tempura (tori-ten) is juicy in a way that surprises people who expected a compromise — this bowl has its own fans, not just its own budget.

**Yasai Tendon — the vegetable bowl (730 yen).** Six vegetables and nothing else: maitake, eggplant, lotus root, sweet potato, pumpkin, and green beans. Sweet potato and pumpkin bring a sweetness the seafood bowls do not have, and maitake carries more flavor than most mushrooms manage. The order for anyone skipping seafood, and a genuinely different bowl rather than a stripped one.

**Kaisen Kakiage Tendon — the mound (960 yen).** Instead of separate pieces, one large kakiage — a fritter of peeled shrimp, itaya scallops, lotus root, and mitsuba herbs fried together into a single lacy disc — laid over the rice. Bigger, richer, and more chaotic than the lined-up bowls; every bite has everything in it. Regulars treat it as the heavy option.

**Beyond the bowls.** The same seven lineups return as soba sets (from 880 yen for the chicken and maitake with a small soba) and teishoku sets (from 830 yen), as listed in the system section above. Single pieces let you build: an extra shrimp (¥290), a whole kakiage (¥760), or a ¥100 piece of pumpkin to round a bowl out. And the menu has a drinking corner — the **draft beer set (800 yen)** pairs four pieces of tempura with a draft beer or sake, because fried food and a cold beer at 6 p.m. is a use case the chain takes seriously. Seasonal limited bowls rotate through the year on top of all this, and the chain runs periodic appreciation campaigns (the official site announces them), which is when regulars stock up on discounted takeout bento.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Say your sauce preference when you order, not after.** The tare goes on as the bowl is assembled. Extra or light are both normal — after the bowl arrives, the moment has passed.
- **Eat it promptly.** Tendon is a race between crisp and soaked by design, but a bowl left sitting while you take twenty photos loses on both counts. One photo, then eat.
- **Leave the bowls where they are.** At Tenya the staff clear the tables. Do not carry your bowl looking for a return shelf — there isn't one, and the old comparison habit from self-service udon shops does not apply here.
- **No tipping.** Staff cannot accept it; coins left on the table will be treated as forgotten change. The exact price plus "gochisousama deshita" is the complete and correct thank-you.
- **Counter seats are a flow, not a lounge.** At lunch peak, eat, pay, and give the seat up. Off-peak, nobody will rush you.
- **Fresh-fried means a short wait.** Complaining that the food takes a few minutes marks you as not understanding what you ordered — the frying starts when your order lands.

## Why locals are satisfied

The satisfaction at Tenya is the sound. A tendon arrives still audibly crisp, the sauce steaming where it hits the hot batter, and the first bite has the crunch that tempura restaurants charge three times as much to produce. The chain industrialized the frying — batter, oil, and timing standardized until a part-time cook at a station store produces the same bowl as the flagship — without industrializing the eating. You sit, the bowl is yours, the miso soup is hot, and the whole thing costs what a convenience-store lunch costs. That is the position Tenya holds in everyday Tokyo life: the small celebration that fits inside a weekday, the "a little better than usual" that never needs a special occasion to justify it.
