Every visitor to Japan eventually has the same small emergency: a broken umbrella, a missing cable, an extra bag for souvenirs, a rainy-day activity for a child. Locals solve all of these the same way — they walk into a 100-yen shop. There are thousands of them, they sit inside stations and on every shopping street, and three chains account for most of them.

From the street, Daiso, Seria, and Can Do look interchangeable: bright aisles, low prices, everything from chopsticks to screwdrivers. Inside, they are not the same shop at all. Each chain has a different idea of what a 100-yen shop is for — and once you know the split, you will choose between them the way locals do.

## What is a 100-yen shop?

A 100-yen shop sells everyday goods at a uniform price: 100 yen per item, before tax. The idea is older than the current chains — it grew out of travelling discount stalls and took its modern form in the 1990s, when Daiso industrialized it: design or commission products in huge volumes, keep the price fixed, and let the fixed price itself do the marketing. No sale banners, no mental arithmetic. If it is on the shelf, it is (mostly) 100 yen.

Two things surprise first-time visitors.

**Not everything is 100 yen.** All three chains now run higher-priced lines inside the same store — 200, 300, 500, and 1,000 yen items, marked on the tag. Daiso has gone furthest, with separate premium formats, but even an ordinary store will have a shelf of electronics or large storage that costs more. The uniform price is the center of gravity, not a promise about every single item.

**The quality is the real story.** These are not party-favor goods. Because the chains sell in enormous volume, manufacturers engineer products to a fixed cost with genuine care: the storage boxes stack properly, the kitchen tools survive the dishwasher, the stationery writes smoothly. Japanese homes quietly run on this stuff — drawer organizers, drain nets, travel bottles, spare everything. The honest limits are durability under hard use and precision: a 100-yen screwdriver is for a loose hinge, not for building furniture.

## When do people actually use them?

Locals do not "go shopping" at a 100-yen shop the way they visit a department store. They use them as infrastructure, in specific situations:

- **Something is missing, right now.** A cable, an umbrella, batteries, a sewing needle, a lock for a suitcase. On a trip, this is the traveler's version: the shop near the station replaces whatever the hotel room or your luggage failed to provide.
- **Setting up a home.** Moving house in Japan reliably involves a 100-yen shop run: storage, hangers, cleaning supplies, kitchen basics — a whole starter kit for the price of one regular-store item.
- **Souvenirs and small gifts.** Chopsticks, fans, character goods, Japanese-pattern pouches, snacks. Light, flat, cheap, and genuinely used back home — locals buy omiyage extras here too.
- **Crafts and hobbies.** Seria especially: yarn, resin, beads, miniature materials. Whole hobby communities are built on 100-yen materials.
- **Seasonal and disaster goods.** Rain gear in June, fans in August, and a permanent corner of emergency supplies — lights, gloves, water containers — because in Japan preparedness is an everyday purchase, not a special one.

## Daiso: the maximum store

Daiso is the largest of the three by a wide margin, and it behaves like it. Flagship stores run to several floors and tens of thousands of items: food and drink, travel goods, tools, gardening, pet supplies, cosmetics, electronics accessories, and seasonal corners that turn over completely with the calendar. If a product category exists in daily life, Daiso probably has a 100-yen version of it — and, increasingly, a 300- or 500-yen version beside it.

**What to know.** Daiso is the chain for a list. Travelers stock up on compression bags, laundry nets, and snack souvenirs; locals furnish kitchens and desks. The trade-off for the range is decision fatigue: give yourself a list before you walk in, or you will leave with twelve things you did not know existed. Prices above 100 yen are clearly tagged — check tags on anything large or electronic.

## Seria: the beautiful one

Seria built its name on a different promise: everything is 100 yen, and it should not look like it. The stores feel calmer and more curated — natural materials, muted colors, tableware you would set out for guests, and interior goods that blend into a home instead of shouting their price. Its handicraft section is the strongest of the three: yarn, fabric, resin and bead materials, miniature and doll-house supplies, with a community of makers who treat new Seria stock as an event.

**What to know.** Seria is where locals buy things they will see every day, and where travelers find gifts that do not look like airport souvenirs. The range is narrower than Daiso's — less food, fewer tools — so it pairs with, rather than replaces, a Daiso run. If you are choosing souvenirs for people whose taste you respect, start here.

## Can Do: the neighborhood one

Can Do is the smallest of the three chains, and the most embedded in ordinary life: compact stores inside station buildings and local shopping streets, stocked tight with daily necessities — stationery, cleaning supplies, kitchen basics, seasonal needs. It does not try to be a destination. It tries to be on your way home.

**What to know.** For a traveler, Can Do is often simply the closest one: the umbrella when the rain starts, the tote bag when the souvenirs overflow, the batteries tonight rather than tomorrow. Its smaller range is the point — you are in and out in five minutes with exactly the thing you came for.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Do not photograph the store casually.** Rules vary by store, and other shoppers did not agree to be in your video. Photograph your purchases after you buy them, outside the store. If you truly need a shelf photo, ask staff first and accept the answer.
- **Never open packages before paying.** Testing is not part of the format: packaging is the product information, and an opened item cannot be sold. If you need to check size or content, the measurements and contents are printed on the package — or ask staff.
- **Bags cost money.** Shopping bags are charged at the register, a few yen each. You will be asked; if you have a tote, say so with a smile and "daijoubu desu".
- **No tipping, here as everywhere in Japan.** Staff cannot accept it, and coins left on the counter will be chased after you as forgotten change.
- **Keep the aisles moving.** Aisles are narrow and the shops are busy. Park your suitcase near the entrance wall, not in the middle of the aisle, and let staff restocking shelves pass.
- **Everything goes back where it belongs.** If you change your mind about an item, return it to its shelf or hand it to staff at the register — do not leave it on a random shelf for someone else to sort.

## How locals actually choose

Honestly, most people start with whichever chain is closest — and then develop loyalties by category. Daiso for the big restock and anything unusual; Seria for the home, the table, and the craft drawer; Can Do for the five-minute fix on the way home. Parents know which chain stocks the current character collaboration; makers follow Seria's new materials; travelers learn that a station-front Daiso can solve almost any small disaster of the trip. The chains compete less on price — the price is the same — than on what kind of everyday life each one imagines for you.
