**About the phrases in this guide:** every phrase below comes as a card. If pronouncing Japanese feels like too much, just open this page and show the card to the staff — you'll be understood. And if you do try saying it out loud, however it comes out, they'll be even happier that you tried.

If gyudon is Japan's rice bowl, udon and soba are its noodles — the meal people eat when they want something hot, fast, and cheap without thinking about it. Station platforms, shopping streets, and office districts all have a noodle counter, and the two big formats are opposites in everything except speed: the self-service udon line, where you build your own tray, and the standing soba shop, where you eat at a counter and leave.

This page compares the three chains a first-time visitor is most likely to meet: Marugame Seimen and Hanamaru Udon for self-service udon, and Fuji Soba for standing soba. The real differences are how you order, how much control you get over what lands in your bowl, and how many minutes the whole thing takes.

## What are udon and soba?

**Udon** are thick wheat noodles, white and pleasantly chewy, served in a clear dashi broth flavored with soy sauce and mirin. The plainest bowl, *kake udon*, is just noodles and broth. From there you add tempura, a raw or soft egg, wakame seaweed, or a slice of fried tofu. Udon comes from all over Japan, but the style the big chains follow is Sanuki udon from Kagawa, famous for firm, springy noodles made fresh in-store.

**Soba** are buckwheat noodles — thinner, darker, with a delicate nutty flavor that disappears if you rush. They are eaten two ways: hot in broth (*kake soba*), or cold on a bamboo tray (*mori soba* or *zaru soba*), dipped bite by bite into a small cup of concentrated sauce. When you finish cold soba, staff may offer *sobayu* — the hot water the noodles were cooked in — to mix with your remaining dipping sauce and drink. It is the soba drinker's dessert.

**The broth has a geography.** In eastern Japan (Kanto, including Tokyo) the dashi is darker and stronger with soy sauce. In western Japan (Kansai) it is paler, lighter, and more dashi-forward. Tokyo chains follow the eastern style, so the soup will look dark and taste assertive — that is correct, not a mistake.

**Two cultures of eating them.** *Self-service udon* is a cafeteria line: you carry a tray, collect your bowl, choose your own tempura, and pay at the end. *Standing soba* (*tachigui*) is even more stripped down: a counter, no chairs, a ticket machine, and customers who treat the whole stop as a pause between trains. Both are complete meals for a few hundred yen, and both assume you already know the flow — which is why this page exists.

## When locals crave it

- **The five-minute lunch.** A bowl of kake udon or soba is what you eat when the lunch break is real but short. Hot, filling enough, and you are back at your desk before anyone notices.
- **The train-transfer stop.** Standing soba lives inside and beside stations because it matches the rhythm of a transfer: ticket, bowl, gone. The 6 p.m. bowl before the last train home is a ritual for a whole class of commuters.
- **The end of a drinking night.** A light bowl of noodles is the classic way to close an evening — easier on the stomach than ramen, and the warm broth feels like a reset.
- **The hot-day answer.** Cold soba and cold udon are summer food. When the heat kills your appetite, cold noodles with a sharp dipping sauce are what still goes down.
- **The gentle day.** After days of heavy eating, or when your stomach wants a rest, plain udon in clear broth is the default recovery meal — the one mothers make for children with colds, scaled up to a national habit.

If your mood is "hot soup, no decisions, ten minutes," you are in an udon-or-soba mood. The only question left is which format — and that is what the rest of this page is for.

## Marugame Seimen: noodles made from flour, in every store

Marugame is the chain that turned the Sanuki self-service format into a national standard, and its defining commitment is easy to state: **the noodles are made from flour, in the store, every day.** Every location mixes, kneads, rests, and cuts its own dough from 100% domestic wheat flour, salt, and water — nothing else — and the chain trains and certifies its own noodle craftsmen (*men shokunin*), who adjust the work to the day's temperature and humidity. At many stores you can watch the dough being made through the glass at the noodle station while you queue. The dashi is prepared in-store as well. Nothing about the bowl arrives finished.

**The flow.** Take a tray at the entrance. At the noodle station, say your order — which udon, which size, hot or cold. Your bowl is made in front of you and placed on your tray. Then you slide along the tempura line and pick what you want with the tongs: a shrimp tempura, a chikuwa (fish cake) tempura, a vegetable kakiage. Each piece is priced individually and goes on a side plate. At the register you pay for the bowl plus whatever you picked. After paying, there is a garnish corner — green onion, grated ginger, tempura crumbs, sesame — all free, all self-service. Eat, then carry your tray to the return shelf.

**The menu, in Marugame's character.** The signature is **kamaage udon** (regular ¥390): noodles lifted straight from the boiling pot into a wooden tub with some of their hot cooking water, dipped bite by bite into a small cup of concentrated broth. There is nowhere for a weak noodle to hide in this bowl, which is exactly why the chain leads with it. The other pillar is **kamatama udon** (regular ¥490) — just-boiled noodles, never rinsed, with a raw egg cracked over them and a swirl of dashi soy sauce, so the heat of the noodles half-cooks the egg into the sauce. **Kake udon** (regular ¥390) is the plain starting bowl, and everything else — bukkake, curry, meat udon — builds on the same fresh noodle.

**What to know.** The base bowl is cheap; the tray is where the bill grows. Two pieces of tempura can cost as much as the noodles. Locals have a usual order and do not browse — decide your tempura before you reach for the tongs, because the line behind you is moving. Takeout is well established here (the udon bento boxes are a whole category), at the same prices as eating in plus a ¥50 container fee per bowl — but the kamatama bowls cannot be taken out at all.

## Hanamaru Udon: the same idea, engineered for consistency

Hanamaru runs the same self-service format as Marugame, but its philosophy is the mirror image. Where Marugame makes noodles in every store, Hanamaru makes them in factories — deliberately, and it publishes the reasoning like an engineering spec. The traditional foot-kneading (*ashibumi*) method is reproduced mechanically so every batch gets the same chew. The flour is a custom "Hanamaru specification" blend using only top-grade first-class flour — the best 60% of the wheat berry. The water is a patented blend of purified and soft water, tuned to match Takamatsu's water so all five factories produce the same noodle, and stores boil the noodles in soft water too. The goal is not a craftsman's best day. It is the same bowl at every store, every day — and the chain says so.

**The flow.** Identical in structure to Marugame: tray, noodle station, tempura and topping line, register, garnish, return shelf. If you learn one of these two chains, you know both. One visible difference in the line: alongside the tempura, many Hanamaru stores keep a simmering **oden** case — daikon radish, egg, and fish cakes in dashi — picked the same self-service way.

**The menu, in Hanamaru's character.** The signature is the plain **kake udon** itself (small ¥360, medium ¥550, large ¥770 — the sizes are one, two, and three balls of noodles). The broth is *iriko dashi*, made from dried sardines: the Sanuki standard, lighter and more delicate than its color suggests. Hanamaru treats this bowl as the flagship and held the small at ¥360 through its 2026 price revision, while other bowls moved. From there, **ontama bukkake** (small ¥490) — noodles with a soft-cooked egg under concentrated broth you pour over yourself, hot or cold — and meatier bowls like the salted pork and green onion udon (small ¥780) carry the menu, with seasonal limited bowls rotating through to keep regulars interested.

**What to know.** Hanamaru is where students and office workers go when the goal is simply "hot noodles, minimum spend." Portions run slightly smaller, which is exactly the point for a light lunch. Choose Hanamaru when it is the closer shop and you want the plain bowl done well; choose Marugame when the tempura selection is the meal. Takeout is the same price as eating in, plus a ¥50 container fee per bowl.

## Fuji Soba: standing, ticket first, gone in ten minutes

Fuji Soba is a Tokyo institution of a different kind: the standing soba shop. Most locations are in or next to stations, the counters have no chairs, and the entire visit is designed around the train timetable.

**The flow.** Buy a meal ticket from the machine by the door — buttons are labeled with the menu, photos on newer machines. Then comes the detail that confuses every first-timer: **most tickets do not fix your noodle.** The menu is written "kake soba (udon)", "tempura soba (udon)" — one dish, two possible noodles, one button. When you hand the ticket across the counter, the staff will ask **"Soba desu ka, udon desu ka?"** — soba or udon? Answer with the phrase card above, or just point and say "soba". Your bowl arrives in a minute or two, often with a call of your order. Eat standing, drink the broth, put your bowl on the counter's return ledge or hand it back, and go.

**The commitment behind the counter.** Fuji Soba behaves like a proper soba shop that happens to be fast. Fresh raw soba is delivered to every store each morning and boiled to order, then rinsed in water to firm it up — the extra step that cheap standing shops, working from pre-boiled noodles, skip. The broth is made without preservatives, the dashi is drawn in-store (fresh-ground dashi machines since 2013, with a seasoning base built on Shodoshima soy sauce), and the tempura has been fried in-store since 2014 at most locations.

**The menu, in Fuji Soba's character.** **Kake soba** (¥450) is the baseline, and **tempura soba** (¥620) the classic upgrade. The cult items are **beni shoga ten soba** (¥620) — a scarlet tangle of pickled red ginger tempura, sharp enough to wake you up between trains — and the **Tokusen Fuji Soba** (¥600), the loaded house special. Then there is the **katsudon** (¥630): a whole single cutlet, egged and finished to order, which the chain itself counts as one of its signatures — a standing soba shop whose rice bowl has a following of its own. Morning-only *asa soba* (morning soba) serves the commuter breakfast shift. The chain notes that prices vary somewhat by area.

**What to know.** The soul of the place is the plain kake soba eaten in the time between two trains. Do not expect to sit, spread out, or stay — the shop's kindness is speed, not comfort. Takeout is the exception here, not the system: some stores sell it and many do not (the official shop list marks which), because the whole format assumes you eat at the counter.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Keep the self-service line moving.** The line is a conveyor and you are on it. Decide your noodles before the noodle station and your tempura before the tongs. If you need a moment, let the person behind you pass — that small wave-through is the local solution.
- **One tray, one person, in order.** Do not reach across other customers for tempura, and do not go back upstream in the line for a piece you missed. What you passed is gone; the register will not wait while you rebuild your tray.
- **Trays and bowls go back.** At self-service udon shops, returning your tray to the return shelf is part of the meal, not a favor. At standing soba, leave the bowl on the counter ledge — do not carry it looking for a bin.
- **Standing shops are a flow, not a rest stop.** Eat, and give the space up. Reading your phone over an empty bowl while a line forms at the ticket machine is the standing shop's one real faux pas.
- **No tipping, anywhere in this article.** Staff cannot accept it, and coins left on the counter will be chased after you as forgotten change. The exact price and a "gochisousama deshita" complete the transaction.
- **Slurp, but do not splash.** Slurping is normal. Lifting noodles too high and showering the counter is not — keep the bowl close and the splash zone small, especially shoulder to shoulder at a standing counter.

## How locals actually choose

Most people are not comparing chains; they are following geography and habit. The split looks like this: Marugame when the tempura line is the point and you want to build your bowl; Hanamaru when the plain bowl at the lowest price is the whole plan; Fuji Soba when the train is in twelve minutes and dinner needs to happen inside that window. Salarymen rotate by the day's schedule, students live at Hanamaru prices, and nobody standing at a Fuji Soba counter is there by accident.
