If gyudon is Japan's rice bowl, udon and soba are its noodles — the meal people eat when they want something hot, fast, and cheap without thinking about it. Station platforms, shopping streets, and office districts all have a noodle counter, and the two big formats are opposites in everything except speed: the self-service udon line, where you build your own tray, and the standing soba shop, where you eat at a counter and leave.

This page compares the three chains a first-time visitor is most likely to meet: Marugame Seimen and Hanamaru Udon for self-service udon, and Fuji Soba for standing soba. The real differences are how you order, how much control you get over what lands in your bowl, and how many minutes the whole thing takes.

## What are udon and soba?

**Udon** are thick wheat noodles, white and pleasantly chewy, served in a clear dashi broth flavored with soy sauce and mirin. The plainest bowl, *kake udon*, is just noodles and broth. From there you add tempura, a raw or soft egg, wakame seaweed, or a slice of fried tofu. Udon comes from all over Japan, but the style the big chains follow is Sanuki udon from Kagawa, famous for firm, springy noodles made fresh in-store.

**Soba** are buckwheat noodles — thinner, darker, with a delicate nutty flavor that disappears if you rush. They are eaten two ways: hot in broth (*kake soba*), or cold on a bamboo tray (*mori soba* or *zaru soba*), dipped bite by bite into a small cup of concentrated sauce. When you finish cold soba, staff may offer *sobayu* — the hot water the noodles were cooked in — to mix with your remaining dipping sauce and drink. It is the soba drinker's dessert.

**The broth has a geography.** In eastern Japan (Kanto, including Tokyo) the dashi is darker and stronger with soy sauce. In western Japan (Kansai) it is paler, lighter, and more dashi-forward. Tokyo chains follow the eastern style, so the soup will look dark and taste assertive — that is correct, not a mistake.

**Two cultures of eating them.** *Self-service udon* is a cafeteria line: you carry a tray, collect your bowl, choose your own tempura, and pay at the end. *Standing soba* (*tachigui*) is even more stripped down: a counter, no chairs, a ticket machine, and customers who treat the whole stop as a pause between trains. Both are complete meals for a few hundred yen, and both assume you already know the flow — which is why this page exists.

## When locals crave it

- **The five-minute lunch.** A bowl of kake udon or soba is what you eat when the lunch break is real but short. Hot, filling enough, and you are back at your desk before anyone notices.
- **The train-transfer stop.** Standing soba lives inside and beside stations because it matches the rhythm of a transfer: ticket, bowl, gone. The 6 p.m. bowl before the last train home is a ritual for a whole class of commuters.
- **The end of a drinking night.** A light bowl of noodles is the classic way to close an evening — easier on the stomach than ramen, and the warm broth feels like a reset.
- **The hot-day answer.** Cold soba and cold udon are summer food. When the heat kills your appetite, cold noodles with a sharp dipping sauce are what still goes down.
- **The gentle day.** After days of heavy eating, or when your stomach wants a rest, plain udon in clear broth is the default recovery meal — the one mothers make for children with colds, scaled up to a national habit.

If your mood is "hot soup, no decisions, ten minutes," you are in an udon-or-soba mood. The only question left is which format — and that is what the rest of this page is for.

## Marugame Seimen: the self-service line, perfected

Marugame is the chain that turned the Sanuki self-service format into a national standard. The noodles are made in-store, and the line is the whole experience.

**The flow.** Take a tray at the entrance. At the noodle station, say your order — which udon, which size, hot or cold. Your bowl is made in front of you and placed on your tray. Then you slide along the tempura line and pick what you want with the tongs: a shrimp tempura, a chikuwa (fish cake) tempura, a vegetable kakiage. Each piece is priced individually and goes on a side plate. At the register you pay for the bowl plus whatever you picked. After paying, there is a garnish corner — green onion, grated ginger, tempura crumbs, sesame — all free, all self-service. Eat, then carry your tray to the return shelf.

**What to know.** The base bowl is cheap; the tray is where the bill grows. Two pieces of tempura can cost as much as the noodles. Locals have a usual order and do not browse — decide your tempura before you reach for the tongs, because the line behind you is moving.

## Hanamaru Udon: the same idea, stripped to the price

Hanamaru runs the same self-service format as Marugame with a tighter menu and a sharper focus on price. The kake udon in a small size is one of the cheapest hot meals in Tokyo, and the chain leans on seasonal and regional limited bowls to keep regulars interested.

**The flow.** Identical in structure to Marugame: tray, noodle station, tempura and topping line, register, garnish, return shelf. If you learn one of these two chains, you know both.

**What to know.** Hanamaru is where students and office workers go when the goal is simply "hot noodles, minimum spend." Portions run slightly smaller, which is exactly the point for a light lunch. Choose Hanamaru when it is the closer shop and you want the plain bowl done well; choose Marugame when the tempura selection is the meal.

## Fuji Soba: standing, ticket first, gone in ten minutes

Fuji Soba is a Tokyo institution of a different kind: the standing soba shop. Most locations are in or next to stations, the counters have no chairs, and the entire visit is designed around the train timetable.

**The flow.** Buy a meal ticket from the machine by the door — buttons are labeled with the menu, photos on newer machines. Hand the ticket to the staff behind the counter and find a spot to stand. Your bowl arrives in a minute or two, often with a call of your order. Eat standing, drink the broth, put your bowl on the counter's return ledge or hand it back, and go.

**What to know.** The menu is wider than it looks: tempura soba, wakame soba, curry, and even small rice bowls and katsudon sets at some shops. But the soul of the place is the plain kake soba eaten in the time between two trains. Do not expect to sit, spread out, or stay — the shop's kindness is speed, not comfort.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Keep the self-service line moving.** The line is a conveyor and you are on it. Decide your noodles before the noodle station and your tempura before the tongs. If you need a moment, let the person behind you pass — that small wave-through is the local solution.
- **One tray, one person, in order.** Do not reach across other customers for tempura, and do not go back upstream in the line for a piece you missed. What you passed is gone; the register will not wait while you rebuild your tray.
- **Trays and bowls go back.** At self-service udon shops, returning your tray to the return shelf is part of the meal, not a favor. At standing soba, leave the bowl on the counter ledge — do not carry it looking for a bin.
- **Standing shops are a flow, not a rest stop.** Eat, and give the space up. Reading your phone over an empty bowl while a line forms at the ticket machine is the standing shop's one real faux pas.
- **No tipping, anywhere in this article.** Staff cannot accept it, and coins left on the counter will be chased after you as forgotten change. The exact price and a "gochisousama deshita" complete the transaction.
- **Slurp, but do not splash.** Slurping is normal. Lifting noodles too high and showering the counter is not — keep the bowl close and the splash zone small, especially shoulder to shoulder at a standing counter.

## How locals actually choose

Most people are not comparing chains; they are following geography and habit. The split looks like this: Marugame when the tempura line is the point and you want to build your bowl; Hanamaru when the plain bowl at the lowest price is the whole plan; Fuji Soba when the train is in twelve minutes and dinner needs to happen inside that window. Salarymen rotate by the day's schedule, students live at Hanamaru prices, and nobody standing at a Fuji Soba counter is there by accident.
