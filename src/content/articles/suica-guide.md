**About the phrases in this guide:** every phrase below comes as a card. If pronouncing Japanese feels like too much, just open this page and show the card to the staff — you'll be understood. And if you do try saying it out loud, however it comes out, they'll be even happier that you tried.

If you learn one system on your first day in Tokyo, make it this one. Suica is the tap card that opens the ticket gates, pays for your bus, buys your coffee at the convenience store, and rents your coin locker. One card, charged once, works almost everywhere a visitor actually goes. This guide follows the whole life of the card: choosing the right type, buying it, charging it, using it correctly, and — just as important — fixing the handful of mistakes that every first-time visitor makes at the gate.

## Step 1: Choose your type — physical, Welcome, or mobile

There are three ways visitors use Suica, and the right choice depends on your phone and how long you are staying.

- **Mobile Suica (recommended if your phone supports it).** iPhone users can create a Suica directly in Apple Wallet and charge it with a registered card — no deposit, no machine, no queue at the airport. Android support depends on the device and where it was sold, so check before relying on it.
- **Welcome Suica.** A visitor-only physical card sold at airports and major stations, at 1,000 to 10,000 yen with the full amount usable. No deposit to track, but two catches: it expires **28 days after purchase** (the purchase day counts), and **any remaining balance is lost** — it cannot be refunded. Charge it in small amounts.
- **Regular physical Suica.** The same card Tokyo residents use. It carries a refundable **500-yen deposit** and the balance can be refunded (minus a fee) at the end of your trip. Note that sales of new physical cards were restricted for a period due to a chip shortage and the situation has changed more than once — treat availability as something to confirm on arrival, not a guarantee from an old guidebook.

## Step 2: Buy it and charge it

At a ticket machine, look for the IC card menu — machines at JR stations sell and charge cards, and the screens have an English option. Charging (topping up) can be done in three places:

1. **Station ticket machines** — insert the card, choose the amount, pay by cash. Some machines accept cards for charging; cash always works.
2. **Convenience stores** — hand your card and cash to the staff and say the charging phrase from this page. Locals do this constantly because there is a konbini on every block.
3. **Your phone** — Mobile Suica charges instantly from your wallet app, which is the easiest way to fix a low balance before you reach the gate.

Charge more than you think you need for the day. A few thousand yen covers trains plus small purchases, and running dry mid-journey is the single most common Suica problem.

**One card per person — no sharing, no double-tapping for two.** A Suica records one person's entry and one person's exit, so a single card cannot carry two riders through a gate. Everyone who pays a fare needs their own card or phone. The details for children: ages 6 to 11 pay the child fare (half the adult fare) and get their own child Suica, sold at ticket offices. **Children under 6 ride free** when traveling with a fare-paying passenger — up to two per adult; from the third small child onward, each additional one needs a child fare. Babies under 1 are always free. So a couple with a toddler needs exactly two cards, not three.

## Step 3: Use the gates — touch in, touch out

The rule is absolute: **touch when you enter, touch when you exit.** The fare is calculated between those two touches. Touch the card flat against the round reader, and keep it there until you hear the beep and see the gate open — about a full second. A quick wave past the reader is the number one cause of failed touches.

Make each touch a two-part check: **listen for the beep, then glance at the small display on the gate.** On exit it shows the fare deducted and your remaining balance. That one-second glance is how you catch a failing balance before it strands you, and how you know the touch actually registered — no beep and no display change means the card was not read, whatever it felt like.

The same touch pays at convenience stores, vending machines (look for the IC reader panel), buses (touch when boarding or exiting depending on the city — in Tokyo, touch when you get off for city buses with flat fares, and follow the local pattern elsewhere), and many coin lockers and taxis.

## Step 4: The failure patterns — and exactly how to fix them

- **Insufficient balance.** The gate beeps, the doors stay shut, and the screen shows the balance error. Do not panic and do not force it. Step aside, and either charge at the fare adjustment machine near the gates or go to the staffed gate and ask for settlement with the phrases on this page.
- **No entry record.** You touched in too quickly and it never registered, so the exit gate has nothing to calculate. Go to the staffed gate, say which station you came from, and staff will fix the record on the spot. This happens to residents too, and station staff handle it politely every day — fixing records is one of the most routine jobs at that window, and you will be treated as a normal case, not a problem.
- **Double touch / wrong gate.** If you touch twice or follow someone through, the card state gets confused. Same fix: staffed gate, explain with a smile.
- **Welcome Suica expiring with money on it.** There is no fix after the fact — the balance is gone. Spend it down at a convenience store before your last day.
- **Lost physical card.** A registered (named) Suica can be reissued with its balance; an anonymous one cannot. If losing the balance would hurt, register the card or go mobile.

In every case, the answer is the same: **the staffed gate, at the side of the gate line, is where card problems are solved.** That is literally what it is for.

## Step 5: Leaving — returning the card and the refund trick

If you bought a regular physical Suica and do not plan to keep it as a souvenir, you can return it at a **JR East Midori no Madoguchi** (the green ticket office) at a station in the Suica area. The refund is simple arithmetic: **your balance, minus a 220-yen handling fee, plus your 500-yen deposit.**

The trick is in the fee. If your remaining balance is **220 yen or less, the fee takes all of it** and you get only the 500-yen deposit back. So before returning the card, spend the balance down — a convenience store run for snacks and drinks on your last day is the standard move. Return the card with a zero or near-zero balance and the deposit comes back in full. (Welcome Suica is different: no deposit, and no refunds at all — its balance must simply be spent.)

## Gate manners and what not to do

The gate line in a Tokyo station moves like a conveyor belt, and the etiquette exists to keep it moving.

**Do:**
- **Have your card or phone out before you reach the gate.** The flow assumes zero fumbling time.
- **Touch firmly and wait for the response.** Flat on the reader, one full second, until the beep.
- **Move aside immediately if something goes wrong.** Fix problems at the fare machine or staffed gate, not in the gate lane.

**Do not:**
- **Stop in front of the gate to search your bag.** Step out of the line first. A stopped person at a Tokyo gate during rush hour blocks dozens of people in seconds.
- **Tailgate through behind the person in front of you.** The gate counts touches, not people — you will end up with no entry record and a problem at your destination.
- **Tap with your card buried in a thick wallet full of other cards.** Other IC cards and transit passes interfere with the reader. Keep Suica on its own, or use your phone.
- **Try to pay a train fare shortfall in coins at the gate.** Shortfalls are settled at the fare adjustment machine or the staffed gate — the automatic gate cannot take your money.

## How locals actually use it

For Tokyo residents, Suica is not a train card — it is the default small wallet. The morning coffee, the convenience store lunch, the vending machine drink on the platform, the locker at the station: all of it is one touch, and the physical card increasingly lives in a phone. It reaches further than visitors expect, too: some game centers take it — Taito Station arcades accept Suica on many of their machines, and other chains have followed in places. Look for the e-money reader on the cabinet; where there is none, coins are still king. Residents keep a few thousand yen on it at all times, charge it at the konbini with their lunch, and barely think about it. That invisibility is the point — the system is trusted because it works the same way, everywhere, every time.

### Column: one card family, many names

Suica is Tokyo's name for something bigger. Japan's ten main IC cards — Suica (JR East), PASMO (Tokyo's private railways and subways), Kitaca (Hokkaido), TOICA and manaca (Nagoya area), ICOCA and PiTaPa (Osaka and Kansai), SUGOCA and nimoca (Kyushu), and Hayakaken (Fukuoka's subway) — are **mutually compatible across the whole country**. A Suica bought in Tokyo taps onto a bus in Kyoto, a tram in Hiroshima, and a register in Fukuoka exactly the same way; a friend's ICOCA from Osaka works in Tokyo identically. The name and the mascot change by region — the tap does not. Only genuinely local rural lines and buses still sit outside the system, and those are the shrinking exception.

### Column: the penguin on the card — and its retirement

The face of the physical card is a penguin, drawn by illustrator Chihiro Sakazaki, and it has been Suica's mascot since the card launched in 2001. It is also, unexpectedly, news: **the penguin graduates on March 31, 2027**, after 25 years. JR East chose the successor by public vote — about 1.66 million people voted — and the winner is a **purple rabbit**, which takes over as the face of Suica from April 1, 2027. Its nickname is being crowdsourced and will be announced at the handover ceremony on the penguin's last day.

Practical notes for visitors: penguin-design cards are on sale only while stock lasts, and after the changeover new physical cards will be plain. A penguin card you already own keeps working exactly as before — the graduation is about the artwork, not the chip. And despite a rumor you may see online, the penguin has not been given an airport job: JR East has announced no next role for it (the penguin goods sold at Haneda Airport are a different penguin by the same illustrator). JR East's own wording is that in March 2027 the penguin "flies off to a new place."
