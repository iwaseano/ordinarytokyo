Ramen is the Japanese food most visitors arrive already dreaming about — and the one where the ordering systems differ the most from shop to shop. Japan's big ramen chains have each turned ordering into a small ceremony of their own: a ticket machine and a written order sheet at one, a relaxed restaurant menu at another, a single thick broth with a cult following at the third.

This page compares the three chains a first-time visitor is most likely to meet in Tokyo — Ichiran, Ippudo, and Tenkaippin — on how you order, what the signature bowl actually is, and which one fits which moment.

## What is ramen?

Ramen is a bowl built from three parts, and every shop's identity lives in how it balances them:

1. **The broth (suupu).** The soul of the bowl. It can be pork-bone *tonkotsu* (milky, rich), soy-sauce *shoyu* (clear, savory), miso (deep, fermented), salt *shio* (light), or chicken-based. Broth is simmered for hours and is the part the shop is proudest of.
2. **The noodles (men).** Wheat noodles, usually with an alkaline water called *kansui* that gives them their springy bite and yellow tint. Thickness and firmness vary by region and shop — thin and straight for Hakata-style tonkotsu, thick and wavy for Sapporo miso.
3. **The toppings (gu).** Sliced roast pork (*chashu*), seasoned egg (*ajitama*), spring onions, bamboo shoots (*menma*), nori seaweed, and pickled ginger, depending on the style.

On top of that base, Japan has grown whole subcultures: *iekei* (a Yokohama-born tonkotsu-shoyu hybrid eaten with rice), *Jiro-style* (mountainous portions with garlic and extra fat, ordered through a call-and-response of preferences), tsukemen (cold noodles dipped into a separate concentrated broth), and more. The three chains below are the polished, nationwide end of that world — the right place to learn the basics before the deep end.

## When locals crave ramen

Ramen is not an everyday food for most people — it is a *craving* food, and the craving has shapes:

- **After drinking.** A bowl at the end of a night out (*shime no ramen*) is a Tokyo institution.
- **On a cold or rainy day.** Hot broth is weather food.
- **When you want one specific flavor.** People do not crave "ramen" in general; they crave *that* shop's bowl. Chains exist to make that bowl identical every time.
- **For a fast, solo lunch.** A ramen counter is one of the easiest places in Japan to eat alone — nobody notices, nobody minds.
- **When rich is the point.** Some days you want the thickest broth available. That is a Tenkaippin day.

## Ichiran: the booth, the sheet, the silence

Ichiran serves one thing — tonkotsu ramen — and has built an entire system around letting you tune it and eat it undisturbed.

**The flow.** Buy a meal ticket from the machine at the entrance (cash-friendly; choose the base bowl and any extras like kaedama or egg). Check the seat-availability board, and take an open booth. Fill in the **order sheet**: circle your choices for broth richness, oiliness, garlic, spring onion, chashu, the signature spicy sauce (start low — it builds), and noodle firmness. Hand the sheet and your ticket through the opening in front of you. A curtain, a pair of hands, and your bowl arrives. You eat facing a wall, in a partitioned booth, with a tap for water at your seat.

**What to know.** The booths are the point, not a gimmick: Ichiran calls it the flavor-concentration counter, and it genuinely removes every social friction of eating alone in a foreign country. Extra noodles (kaedama) are ordered by pressing the call button and handing over the small metal plate or extra ticket, depending on the store. When you finish, you just stand up and leave — payment already happened.

## Ippudo: tonkotsu as a sit-down restaurant

Ippudo is the chain that took Hakata tonkotsu worldwide, and its stores feel like casual restaurants rather than ramen counters.

**The flow.** Sit down first. Order from the menu — the two anchors are **Shiromaru**, the classic smooth tonkotsu, and **Akamaru**, a modern version layered with flavored oil and miso paste. You will be asked about noodle firmness; *katame* (firm) is the house instinct. Free small sides such as pickled vegetables and bean sprouts are often on the table, and kaedama can be ordered verbally at any point while broth remains. Pay at the register on your way out.

**What to know.** Of the three, this is the chain for people who want to sit across from each other and talk. The broth is rich but rounded — a deliberate step gentler than Tenkaippin — which makes Ippudo the common recommendation for a first tonkotsu.

## Tenkaippin: the thickest broth in the building

Tenkaippin, from Kyoto, is built around one word: **kotteri**. The broth — chicken and vegetables simmered down until it is closer to a sauce than a soup — coats the noodles and the spoon.

**The flow.** Order at your seat or the counter. The one real decision is *kotteri* (thick) versus *assari* (a light, clear broth) — with a middle option at some stores. Everything else is toppings and side dishes like fried rice and gyoza. Pay at the register.

**What to know.** Kotteri divides people permanently: some find it the most satisfying bowl in Japan, others find it heavy after five spoonfuls. The honest advice is to order it once, in the standard size, and find out which person you are. Locals who love it do not describe it as ramen so much as its own category.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Slurp. Really.** Noodles are eaten with an audible slurp — it cools them, mixes in air, and is simply how ramen is eaten. Lifting noodles silently and waiting for them to cool is the foreign move.
- **Chopsticks in one hand, spoon in the other.** The Chinese-style spoon (*renge*) is for broth and small toppings. Use the chopsticks to lift noodles into the spoon or straight to your mouth; drink the last broth from the bowl if you like — finishing it is a compliment, not a requirement.
- **The counter is a flow.** Ramen shops turn seats over quickly. Eat while it is hot, and when the bowl is done, the meal is done. Lingering over an empty bowl at a crowded counter — phone out, no order — is the one habit locals notice.
- **Kaedama has a timing.** Order your noodle refill while broth remains, ideally when you are about halfway through the noodles. It arrives fast; drop it in, loosen it, keep eating.
- **No tipping.** Not at the counter, not at the register. The exact price is the whole transaction.
- **Keep the counter small.** Bag on the hook or under the counter, elbows in. The person next to you is closer than you think — especially at Ichiran, where the dividers do the social work for you.

## How locals actually choose

Habit and craving, in that order. Ichiran is the solo default — the bowl you can have at midnight, in silence, tuned exactly to your taste, and its order sheet makes it the chain foreigners and locals alike use as a benchmark. Ippudo is the group and date-safe choice: same tonkotsu family, but a room you can talk in. Tenkaippin is a loyalty, not a compromise — people who say "kotteri" the way others say a football team. And almost nobody is loyal to only one: the rotation follows the mood, which is why all three can survive on the same street.
