Tempura has an image problem abroad: it reads as a special-occasion food, something ordered piece by piece at a quiet counter. In Tokyo daily life, tempura is also a fast lunch in a bowl, eaten in fifteen minutes at a chain store between trains. The chains on this page — Tenya and Santen — exist to make tendon, the tempura rice bowl, as ordinary and affordable as a beef bowl. This page compares them on how you order, what the base price gets you, and which one fits which moment.

## First, what is donburi?

*Donburi* is the family all of these bowls belong to, and the word means two things at once: the deep, wide bowl itself, and the dish served in it — steamed white rice with something cooked placed on top.

The design is deliberate. The topping is almost always saucy, and the sauce is meant to soak down into the top layer of rice, so every bite carries both. You eat with chopsticks (a spoon appears for some bowls), and lifting the bowl with one hand is normal table manners here, not a shortcut. A donburi is a complete meal in one vessel: protein, rice, and sauce, usually with miso soup and pickles beside it, at a price and speed built for a working lunch.

Once you see the pattern, the whole family reads easily:

- **Gyudon** — beef and onion simmered in sweet soy dashi
- **Katsudon** — a pork cutlet simmered with egg
- **Oyakodon** — chicken and egg, literally "parent and child"
- **Tendon** — tempura over rice with a sweet sauce (this page)
- **Kaisendon** — raw seafood over rice, the celebratory end of the family
- **Unadon** — grilled eel with a rich glaze

Chains specialize. The chains below compete inside one branch of the family — but the bowl underneath never changes.

## What is tendon, and what is tempura?

Tempura is seafood and vegetables coated in a light, cold batter and deep-fried for a short time, so the coating turns crisp and pale gold while the inside steams. Done well, it is not greasy: the batter is a thin shell, not a crust.

**Tendon** takes that tempura and builds a rice bowl out of it. A standard bowl carries two shrimp, a piece of white fish or squid, and seasonal vegetables — pumpkin, green beans, lotus root, a sheet of nori — arranged over rice and finished with *tendon tare*, a sweet, dark soy-based sauce poured over the top. The sauce softens the batter where it touches and seasons the rice below, which is exactly the donburi design: crisp and soaked in the same bite.

At a tendon chain, the tempura is fried to order in front of you, and the whole bowl costs about the same as a gyudon set. That price is the point — tendon chains turned a former treat into a weekday lunch.

## When locals crave tendon

- **When plain rice bowls start to feel repetitive.** Gyudon and katsudon are simmered and soft; tendon is the donburi with crunch. People rotate to it for texture.
- **On a day that deserves a small upgrade.** Shrimp tempura still feels slightly festive, so tendon is the "a little better than usual" lunch that stays within a coin or two of the cheapest options.
- **When you want fried food without a heavy commitment.** A tendon is fried but portioned — one bowl, miso soup, done. It scratches the fried-food itch at lunchtime without a full tonkatsu set.
- **In season.** Chains rotate limited bowls with seasonal vegetables and seafood, and regulars genuinely follow them.

If your mood is "something crisp, something a bit celebratory, still fast and cheap," you are in a tendon mood.

## Tenya: the tendon specialist

Tenya is the chain that built this category and still defines it. The menu is broad for a fast bowl shop: the standard tendon, larger shrimp bowls, sets with soba or udon, and seasonal limited bowls that change through the year. Many stores fry in view of the counter, so your bowl arrives within minutes, still crackling.

**The flow.** Stores vary: some take your order at the counter, others use a ticket machine, and payment is usually at the register. Follow the locals in front of you — the pattern is visible in one visit.

**What to know.** Tenya is where tendon beginners should start. The standard bowl shows the canonical lineup (shrimp, fish, squid, vegetables), and the seasonal bowls are where the chain's regulars actually live.

## Santen: tendon at the lowest price

Santen's proposition is a single sentence: a proper tendon for less. The menu is deliberately short — a few bowls, a few sets — and the operation is stripped down so the price can stay at the bottom of the category.

**The flow.** Ticket machine first at most stores: decide from the board, buy the ticket, hand it over, and the bowl follows quickly.

**What to know.** You trade variety for price. If you already know you want a standard tendon and nothing else, Santen gives it to you for the least money of any chain in this family.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Fresh-fried means a short wait.** Tempura is fried to order, so a few minutes at the counter is the system working, not a delay. Complaining about it marks you as not understanding what you ordered.
- **Say your sauce preference when you order, not after.** The tare goes on as the bowl is assembled. "Tare o oome de" (extra sauce) or "sukuname de" (light) are both normal requests — after the bowl arrives, the moment has passed.
- **No tipping, anywhere in this article.** Staff cannot accept it; coins left behind will be treated as forgotten change. The exact price plus "gochisousama deshita" is the complete and correct thank-you.
- **Counter seats are a flow, not a lounge.** At lunch peak, eat, pay, and give the seat up.
- **Decide before the ticket machine.** Step aside, read the board, then buy. The line's patience is real but not infinite.
- **Eat it promptly.** Tendon is a race between crisp and soaked by design, but a bowl left sitting while you take twenty photos loses on both counts. One photo, then eat.

## How locals actually choose

Most people pick by what is nearest and what they already know. Beyond habit: Tenya when you want the standard, the seasonal special, or a set with noodles; Santen when the budget is the whole decision and a classic tendon is all you need. Office workers treat Tenya as the default tendon and Santen as the end-of-the-month answer, and both chains are full at lunch for exactly those reasons.
