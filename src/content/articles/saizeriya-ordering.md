Ask a Tokyo local where they ate last weekend and Saizeriya comes up constantly — after school with friends, after work with colleagues, on Sunday with three generations of family in one booth. It is an Italian restaurant the way Japan does Italian: sautéed greens with a soft egg, escargot in garlic butter, a baked rice doria under béchamel, and a drink bar that never empties. Almost everything costs a few hundred yen.

It is also one of the easiest restaurants in Japan to use with no Japanese at all, because the ordering system was designed to save labor, not to test you. This page follows one meal in order: how the paper sheet works, what staff will ask, and why locals keep coming back.

## How ordering works, step by step

1. **Wait at the entrance to be seated.** Staff will ask how many people, and possibly whether you smoke, then lead you to a table. Menus and the paper order sheet are already there, or arrive with you.
2. **Decide from the photo menu.** Every item has a big photo and a **menu number**. The number is the whole system — you never need to pronounce the dish name.
3. **Write your order on the paper sheet.** For each item, write the menu number and the quantity in the boxes. One sheet covers the whole table. If you want the drink bar, it has a number too — write it like any other item.
4. **Press the call button on the table.** A small button (often with a bell icon) summons staff. No waving, no calling out. Press it once and wait.
5. **Hand the sheet to the staff.** They read it back to confirm — this is when you hear "ijou de yoroshii desu ka?" (will that be everything?). Nod, and the sheet stays on your table afterward: it is also your running bill.
6. **Food arrives in stages.** Starters and salads first, mains after. The drink bar cup comes early; from then on, refill it yourself at the drink counter whenever you like.
7. **Pay at the register by the entrance.** When you are done, take the order sheet to the register, pay, and leave. Nobody brings a bill to your table, and nobody is waiting for you to free it up in a hurry.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Do not tip.** There is no tipping in Japan, and a chain register has no way to accept one. Coins left on the table will be treated as forgotten change. Pay the exact amount on the sheet and say "gochisousama deshita" — that is the complete transaction.
- **Do not shout for staff.** Calling "sumimasen!" across the room in a loud voice is the one move that marks you as new. The call button exists on every table precisely so nobody has to. Press it, and staff will come.
- **Press the call button once.** One press registers. Repeated pressing does not bring anyone faster and the chime carries across the floor.
- **Staying a while is normal — within reason.** Saizeriya is famous for letting students and families linger over a drink bar for hours, and nobody will rush you. The unspoken line is the queue at the entrance: if people are waiting and you finished long ago, that is the moment to wrap up.
- **One drink bar, one person.** Sharing a single drink bar cup between two people is treated the same as not paying for one of you. Order one per person — it is one of the cheapest items on the menu anyway.
- **Keep voices at café level.** Booths are close together and the room is full of families and people studying. It is a relaxed place, not a quiet one, but loud video calls and speakerphone are out of place.

## Why locals are satisfied

The satisfaction at Saizeriya is not "good for the price" — locals will tell you it is simply good, and the price is the joke on top. The doria arrives bubbling, the spinach is properly garlicky, the drink bar coffee is real coffee. Because the menu barely changes and the quality barely moves, it becomes infrastructure: the place you default to when nobody can decide, when the kids are hungry now, when payday is still a week away. Students grow up in these booths and bring their own children back. That is the position Saizeriya holds in everyday Tokyo life — not a treat, a utility.
