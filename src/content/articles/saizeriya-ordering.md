**About the phrases in this guide:** every phrase below comes as a card. If pronouncing Japanese feels like too much, just open this page and show the card to the staff — you'll be understood. And if you do try saying it out loud, however it comes out, they'll be even happier that you tried.

Ask a Tokyo local where they ate last weekend and Saizeriya comes up constantly — after school with friends, after work with colleagues, on Sunday with three generations of family in one booth. It is an Italian restaurant the way Japan does Italian: a baked rice doria under béchamel, spicy chicken wings, escargot in garlic butter, and a drink bar that never empties.

And then there is the price, which is the real reason this page exists. A glass of wine costs **100 yen**. The most famous dish on the menu, the Milano-style doria, costs **320 yen** — and it was 300 yen for years, until a price revision in October 2026. Almost everything else sits in the few-hundred-yen range, so a full meal — main, side, dessert, and unlimited drinks — routinely comes to less than a single mid-range dish elsewhere. (Those price points are from the chain's official site as of the date above; the data table is updated with the prices actually paid when this page is field-checked.) Locals do not treat Saizeriya as a compromise. They treat the prices as a kind of magic trick they never get tired of.

It is also one of the easiest restaurants in Japan to use with no Japanese at all — once you know the two systems that surprise visitors: ordering by QR code, and the drink bar. This page follows one meal in order.

## How ordering works, step by step

1. **Wait at the entrance to be seated.** Staff will ask how many people ("nan-mei-sama desu ka?") and lead you to a table. All seats in all Japanese stores are non-smoking now, so there is nothing to choose or ask about smoking.
2. **Find the QR code on your table.** It sits on a card or sticker on the table. This code is the ordering system: there is no waiter coming to take your order.
3. **Scan it with your smartphone.** The ordering page opens in your browser — no app and no account needed. Browse the menu, choose items and quantities, and send the order. The kitchen starts cooking immediately.
4. **Order the drink bar the same way, if you want it.** Add it like any other item and a cup arrives at your table. What the drink bar is — and its rules — gets its own section below, because most visitors have never met one.
5. **Food arrives in stages.** Starters and salads first, mains after. Want more? Scan the code again and add items whenever you like; everything accumulates on the table's single bill.
6. **No smartphone? Use the call bell.** Every table also has a small call bell button. Press it once, and when staff arrive, say you have no smartphone ("sumaho ga nai no desu ga, chokusetsu chuumon dekimasu ka?"). They will take your order in person from the paper menu. Same food, same prices.
7. **Pay at the register by the entrance.** When you are done, head to the register, give your table number if asked, and pay. Nobody brings a bill to your table, and nobody is waiting for you to free it up in a hurry.

## The drink bar, explained

The drink bar is a Japanese institution that most visitors have no reference for: a **flat-rate, all-you-can-drink, self-service drink counter** inside the restaurant. You do not order drinks one by one. You pay one set price, receive one cup, and from then on you pour everything yourself.

At the counter you will find a soda fountain (cola, melon soda, and other standards), juices, teas, and a coffee machine for espresso, cappuccino, and regular coffee. Refills are unlimited for the whole visit — locals settle in with a book or homework and refill for hours, and that is considered normal use, not abuse.

The rules are few but firm:

- **One drink bar order is for one person.** Sharing one cup between two people is treated the same as not paying for one of you. It is one of the cheapest items on the menu — order one per person.
- **Your cup stays in the restaurant.** Do not fill a water bottle, a travel mug, or a takeaway cup from the machines. What you pour is for drinking here.
- **Refills mean the counter, not the table.** There are no jugs or free water refills brought by staff — when your cup is empty, the counter is where you go.
- **Take a fresh cup only as the store directs.** Use the cup you were given; if you want a different drink, rinsing or reusing your cup is normal.

## While you wait: the spot-the-difference on the kids' menu

Here is the ritual locals build around the wait. Once your order is sent, go and set up your drinks at the drink bar — then look at the **kids' menu**. Printed on it is a spot-the-difference puzzle: two nearly identical illustrations, with **10 differences** hidden between them.

It is officially for children. Do not be fooled. Finding all 10 is genuinely hard, and adults routinely fail to finish before the food arrives — the last two or three differences are famous for defeating grown-ups with engineering degrees. Groups pass the menu around the table, argue about whether a tiny line counts, and the wait for the doria disappears into the argument.

So the local sequence is: order by QR, build your drink at the drink bar, then hunt for the 10 differences while the kitchen works. If your food arrives with two differences still unfound, you have experienced Saizeriya exactly as Japanese families do.

## The dishes locals actually order

Because this page is about one chain, the menu deserves the spotlight. These are the staples — the items Japanese regulars order on reflex, and the ones a first visit should be built around.

**Milano-style doria (the icon, 320 yen).** A small oval dish of rice under meat sauce and béchamel, baked until the top bubbles and browns. It is not a dish you would find in Milan — it is a Japanese invention wearing an Italian name — and it is the single most famous item in the chain. Rich, hot, and filling, at a price that still makes locals shake their heads. If you order one thing, order this.

**Grilled chicken, diabola style.** A whole grilled chicken thigh with a spicy, garlicky diabola sauce and a pile of vegetables on a sizzling plate. It is the standard "proper main" order — the dish people choose when the doria alone feels too small — and the sauce over rice is half the point.

**Spicy chicken.** Bone-in chicken wings in a spicy glaze, ordered as a side or a drinking snack. This is the table's shared plate: it arrives early, it goes with beer and wine, and regulars order it without looking at the menu.

**Tiramisu.** The default dessert. Saizeriya's tiramisu is a long-running staple made to an Italian recipe, and at this price locals order it the way other people order a coffee after a meal — automatically, to close.

**A glass of wine (still 100 yen).** Red or white, poured generously, and priced lower than the soft drinks in many restaurants. There is also a small decanter for the table. Ordering wine here is not a splurge decision; it is the cheapest line on the drinks page, and locals treat it that way.

Build a first meal the local way: spicy chicken to start, a Milano doria or the diabola chicken as the main, the drink bar running throughout, tiramisu and an espresso to finish. You will still likely pay less than one dish at an ordinary Italian restaurant.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Do not tip.** There is no tipping in Japan, and a chain register has no way to accept one. Coins left on the table will be treated as forgotten change. Pay the exact amount and say "gochisousama deshita" — that is the complete transaction.
- **Do not shout for staff.** Calling "sumimasen!" across the room in a loud voice is the one move that marks you as new. The call bell exists on every table precisely so nobody has to. Press it once, and staff will come.
- **Press the call bell once.** One press registers. Repeated pressing does not bring anyone faster and the chime carries across the floor.
- **Smoking is simply not an option.** Every seat in every store in Japan is non-smoking. Do not ask for a smoking section — there isn't one.
- **Staying a while is normal — within reason.** Saizeriya is famous for letting students and families linger over a drink bar for hours, and nobody will rush you. The unspoken line is the queue at the entrance: if people are waiting and you finished long ago, that is the moment to wrap up.
- **Keep voices at café level.** Booths are close together and the room is full of families and people studying. It is a relaxed place, not a quiet one, but loud video calls and speakerphone are out of place.

## Why locals are satisfied

The satisfaction at Saizeriya is not "good for the price" — locals will tell you it is simply good, and the price is the joke on top. The doria arrives bubbling, the chicken is properly grilled, the drink bar coffee is real coffee. A family of four eats a full meal here for what one person might spend at a mid-range restaurant, and nobody at the table feels they settled. Because the menu barely changes and the quality barely moves, the chain becomes infrastructure: the place you default to when nobody can decide, when the kids are hungry now, when payday is still a week away. Students grow up in these booths and bring their own children back. That is the position Saizeriya holds in everyday Tokyo life — not a treat, a utility.
