Every neighborhood in Tokyo has a moment, around seven in the evening, when the izakaya signs switch on and the first round of draft beer goes out. The izakaya — Japan's casual drinking-and-eating house — is where coworkers decompress, where old friends meet, and where a huge share of ordinary Japanese social life actually happens. It is not a bar with snacks, and it is not a restaurant that happens to serve beer. It is its own institution, with its own rules.

The big chains make the institution easy to enter. This page compares three of the most useful: Torikizoku, where every single item on the menu costs the same; Isomaru Suisan, where you grill shellfish on a burner at your own table; and Shirokiya, the veteran big-room izakaya built for groups. They differ in price structure, ordering style, and mood — and locals choose between them the way they choose between kinds of evening.

## What is an izakaya?

An izakaya is a place built around drinks, with food designed to accompany them. The food comes as many small plates — grilled skewers, sashimi, fried things, simmered things, a rice or noodle dish to finish — shared by the table and ordered in waves over one to three hours. Nobody orders a main course. The table orders a little, drinks, talks, orders a little more.

Three pieces of machinery run almost every izakaya, and all three surprise first-time visitors:

- **Otoshi.** The moment you sit down, a small dish you did not order appears — a little salad, a simmered vegetable, something pickled. This is otoshi, and it is also your seat charge, usually a few hundred yen per person added to the bill. It is not a scam and not optional in spirit: it is how the house prices a table that may sit for three hours. Eat it; it is often genuinely good.
- **Order a little at a time.** The menu is long on purpose. The rhythm is two or three plates per round, not a single giant order. Food arrives when it is ready, in no particular course order, and the last round is traditionally something starchy — fried rice, noodles, or ochazuke (rice with tea) — called *shime*, the closer.
- **Nomihoudai.** Many izakaya offer all-you-can-drink: a fixed price, a set drink list, a time window of roughly two hours. It suits groups that will drink steadily; it is poor value for a light drinker, and the whole table generally has to join the same plan.

Around those three, the customs are simple: the first drink order happens fast ("toriaezu" — for now), nobody touches their glass until the whole table does the kanpai toast, and the bill is one shared total at the end.

## When locals are in the mood for an izakaya

An izakaya is not where locals go when they are hungry in the way a beef bowl is. It is where they go when the point is the people at the table. The moods look like this:

- **The work week just ended.** Friday-night first rounds with coworkers — loud, fast, the week's pressure leaving the room one highball at a time.
- **"We haven't met in months."** Old friends, a table in the corner, and three hours that were supposed to be two.
- **Cheap and cheerful, on purpose.** Students and young workers choosing Torikizoku precisely because the flat price makes the final bill predictable before the first order.
- **A seafood craving with theater.** Isomaru Suisan when the group wants to cook something at the table and smell like the seaside doing it.
- **The big group needs a room.** Year-end parties, welcome parties, farewell parties — the large, booth-divided floor of a Shirokiya, where twenty people can be seated and a course with nomihoudai keeps the budget fixed.

## Torikizoku: everything costs the same

Torikizoku's idea is disarmingly simple: every item on the menu — every skewer plate, every side dish, every drink — costs exactly the same flat price. There is nothing to calculate. You order what sounds good, and the bill is the number of items times one number.

The center of the menu is yakitori: chicken skewers grilled over charcoal, ordered two skewers to a plate, each in *shio* (salt) or *tare* (sweet soy glaze) style. Thigh, skin, meatball (tsukune), chicken with leek — the classics are all here, joined by fried items, salads, rice dishes, and desserts that all obey the same single price. The rooms are bright, wooden, and cheerful, and the crowd skews young.

**The flow.** Sit down, the otoshi arrives, order the first drinks fast, then work through skewers in rounds. At some stores you order on a touch panel; at others, staff take the order. Either way, plates arrive steadily and the table fills up.

**What to know.** Because the price is flat, Torikizoku is where locals go when the group includes someone watching their wallet — nobody has to perform the quiet arithmetic of who ordered the expensive thing. It is also, not coincidentally, one of the easiest first izakaya experiences in Japan.

## Isomaru Suisan: grill it yourself, at the table

Isomaru Suisan is a seafood izakaya built around a small gas burner set into your table. You order shellfish — scallops in the shell, turban shells, squid, crab miso served in the shell — and grill them yourself, with tongs, while the drinks circulate. It is dinner and an activity at the same time, and the smell of grilling shellfish is the chain's signature before you even sit down.

Around the grill there is a full seafood menu: sashimi, grilled fish, fried items, and rice bowls (the chain is also popular at lunch for its seafood donburi). Many stores keep very long hours, some around the clock, which makes Isomaru a fixture of late-night districts.

**The flow.** Otoshi first, drinks, then order the grill items early — they take time, and tending them is the evening's entertainment. Staff will help with timing if you look unsure; watching the neighboring table is the other reliable teacher.

**What to know.** Hamayaki is forgiving but not instant: shellfish are done when they bubble and release from the shell. Do not rush the scallops, and use the tongs, not your chopsticks, for raw items. If raw seafood is new to you, the grill is the gentlest possible introduction — everything you eat, you cooked.

## Shirokiya: the veteran big room

Shirokiya is one of the old names of chain izakaya — a large-format house running since the 1980s, designed for groups. The floors are big, the seating leans toward booths and semi-private spaces, and the menu is the full classic izakaya spread: sashimi, grilled fish, yakitori, fried dishes, salads, hot pots in season, and set courses that bundle food with nomihoudai at a fixed per-person price.

**The flow.** Groups often book a course; walk-ins order from the menu or a tablet. The otoshi arrives, the first round goes out, and the kitchen paces dishes to the table. For parties, the course system removes all decision-making — dishes arrive in sequence and the price was known before you sat down.

**What to know.** Shirokiya is the safe pair of hands: the chain locals book when the group is big, mixed in age, or includes people whose tastes nobody knows. It will not be the most specialized meal of your trip, and that is precisely its job.

## Manners and what not to do

- **The otoshi is not refused.** It arrives unasked and it is charged, and both of those are normal. Treating it as a mistake or sending it back marks you as someone fighting the house's basic system. Eat it — and if you truly cannot eat it, leave it quietly rather than making it a negotiation.
- **Kanpai comes first.** When the first drinks arrive, wait. Nobody drinks until every glass is up and the table says "kanpai" together. Taking a sip early because yours arrived first is the one small rudeness every local notices.
- **Order in rounds, not in one dump.** Two or three plates at a time. A single enormous order jams the kitchen, floods the small table, and goes cold. The izakaya rhythm — order, eat, talk, order again — is the whole design.
- **Keep the volume at your table.** Izakaya are lively and nobody expects quiet — but shouting across the room, singing, or phone speakers carry into every other table's evening. Lively is fine; dominating the room is not.
- **Do not get helplessly drunk.** It sounds obvious, but the nomihoudai clock tempts people to race it. Staff will cut off a table that is disturbing others, and the last train does not wait. Pace the rounds; order the *shime* dish before you think you need it.
- **No tipping.** The otoshi is the seat charge; there is nothing beyond it. Pay the bill as presented, with thanks.
- **Twenty is the legal age.** Drinking (and smoking) in Japan starts at 20, and chains check ID — including, increasingly, visitors'. If anyone at the table is under 20, they order soft drinks, full stop.

## How locals actually choose

The honest split is by the shape of the evening, not by the food alone. Torikizoku when the budget is shared and the mood is skewers and beer — the flat price means the night can never get away from you. Isomaru Suisan when the group wants something to *do* as well as eat, or when the craving is specifically grilled shellfish at midnight. Shirokiya when the group is large, the occasion has a name (welcome, farewell, year-end), and somebody needs the safety of a course price and a room that can hold everyone.

And underneath all three: the chain nearest the station where the group is meeting usually wins. Izakaya loyalty is real, but it is loyalty to an evening that works.
