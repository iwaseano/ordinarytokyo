For most Japanese people, sushi is not a special-occasion food with a chef in a white coat. It is Tuesday. It is the place you take the kids after a sports day, the easy answer when nobody can decide what to eat, the birthday treat that does not require a reservation or a loan. That everyday sushi lives in the conveyor-belt chains — kaiten sushi — and three of them cover the country: Sushiro, Kura Sushi, and Hama Sushi.

All three work on the same basic idea, and all three have quietly become high-tech restaurants where you may not speak to a human until you pay. What differs is the ordering system, how the bill is counted, and the small pieces of theatre each chain adds. This page compares them on those points, with the phrases and manners that make a first visit smooth.

## What is conveyor-belt sushi?

Start with what it is not. It is not the counter sushi of travel posters, where a trained chef shapes each piece in front of you, serves it one at a time, and the bill arrives as a quiet surprise at the end. Conveyor-belt sushi was invented in Osaka in the late 1950s — the story goes that a restaurant owner watched beer bottles moving along a brewery line and saw his staffing problem solved — and it turned sushi from a craft luxury into a self-service meal.

The mechanics are simple. Small plates, usually carrying two pieces of nigiri, travel past your seat on a moving belt. Each plate's color or pattern signals its price. You take what appeals to you, stack the empty plates beside you, and the stack becomes your bill.

Modern chains have changed the balance of that system in two ways. First, **touch-panel ordering**: every seat has a tablet, and most of what you eat is now ordered on it and made fresh. Second, the **express lane**: ordered items arrive on a separate fast lane (or a dedicated track above the main belt) and stop exactly at your seat, announced by a chime or a screen. The slow belt still circles with a reduced selection, but for many diners it has become decoration — a menu you can see, while the real ordering happens on the screen.

One distinction matters, for hygiene and for nerves. The classic image of kaiten sushi — every plate circling past every diner, anyone free to take anything — is not how these three chains mainly work. The shared belt still turns, but with a small, covered selection. The main event is what you order: it is made for you, travels the express lane, and stops at your seat — delivered into your own space, untouched by the room. Some older and smaller sushi shops still run the free-grab belt as the main system; at Sushiro, Kura Sushi, and Hama Sushi, treat the belt as a display case and the lane as your delivery route.

One more piece of the setup matters: tea is free and self-service. Powdered green tea and a hot-water tap sit at every seat. Making your own tea is the first small ritual of the meal, and it is covered in the manners section below.

## When locals crave it

Conveyor-belt sushi fills a specific slot in Japanese life, and it is rarely "I want great sushi." It is:

- **The family negotiation winner.** Grandparents, parents, and a six-year-old who only eats corn sushi can all sit at one table and everyone eats what they like. Few restaurants in Japan absorb disagreement this well.
- **The modest celebration.** Good test results, a birthday, the first day of a holiday — occasions that deserve better than the usual, but not a formal restaurant. A pile of plates feels festive in a way a set meal does not.
- **The "a bit of everything" mood.** Instead of committing to one dish, you graze: two pieces of this, two of that, a bowl of miso soup, a dessert. The small-plate format is the entire point.
- **Payday-adjacent, not payday.** It costs more than a beef bowl and far less than a chef's counter. Locals feel the difference precisely, and choose it when the week has earned a small upgrade.

If your mood is "a proper meal, lots of choice, no formality, and the kids are coming," you are in a conveyor-belt sushi mood. Which chain is a question of systems, not of fish.

## Sushiro: the default answer

Sushiro is the biggest of the three and the one most locals name first. Its base system is the cleanest version of the modern format: check in (by app reservation if you were organised, or at the entrance machine if you were not), sit down, order everything on the touch panel, and collect your plates from the express lane when the chime sounds.

**The flow.** The app matters here more than at the other chains. Weekend lunch at a popular Sushiro can mean a long wait, and the app lets you take a number and watch the queue from outside. Inside, the panel tracks your running total, which makes Sushiro the easiest of the three for a first-timer to budget in real time.

**What to know.** Sushiro's real signature is rotation backed by preparation. The chain's development team pitches new items every week, and campaigns run more than twenty times a year — the menu locals saw last month is not quite the menu you get. Underneath the rotation is an old-fashioned stubbornness about the basics: tuna is the obsession (selected fish, professionally frozen and broken down), yellowtail and sea bream are skinned in-store every day, salmon arrives from selected farms never frozen, and the shari — domestic rice, vinegar folded in while hot — is served at body temperature, switching in some seasons to a red-vinegar version made with aged sake lees. The standard items are the measuring stick, and they are consistent. That consistency at this scale is exactly why it is the default.

## Kura Sushi: the one with the game

Kura Sushi built its identity on two ideas. The first is a hygiene system with a name: **Sendokun**, the chain's antibacterial plate covers. Belt plates travel under individual dome lids that open with a touch, a time-limit system pulls plates that have circled too long, and ordered items arrive sealed in the same spirit. The second is the thing children will tell you about before they mention the fish.

**The plate slot and Bikkura Pon.** At your table there is a slot. When you finish a plate, you drop it in. The slot counts your plates for the bill — practical — and every five plates, the touch panel plays a short game animation. Win, and a capsule toy drops from the machine above your table. Lose, and you feed in five more plates and try again. This is not a minor feature. It converts "eat five more plates" into a family project, and it is the single most effective piece of restaurant theatre in Japan. Parents should know in advance: your children will understand this system instantly and will manage your ordering accordingly.

**What to know.** Kura Sushi also markets what it leaves out — its food is made without four categories of additives (chemical seasonings, artificial sweeteners, synthetic colors, and artificial preservatives), a policy the chain applies across the menu rather than to a token few items. The basics get the same treatment: carefully selected domestic rice, and shari served warm at body temperature, because the chain holds that cold rice ruins good fish. Whether or not that drives your choice, the practical experience is: slightly more ceremony around freshness, the same low base price, and the game.

## Hama Sushi: the relaxed one

Hama Sushi is the third name, run by the same group as Sukiya, and it tends to feel a touch calmer than its rivals. The system is familiar — touch panel, express lane, plates counted at the end — with two local signatures.

**The flow.** At some stores you are greeted and guided to your seat by Pepper, a small humanoid robot, which tells you something about the chain's personality: family-friendly and a little playful without building the whole visit around a game. Ordering and payment follow the standard pattern, with self-checkout spreading store by store.

**What to know.** Hama Sushi's official list of obsessions is four items long, and two of them are unusual for a budget chain: soy sauce and in-store preparation. The tuna is mainly natural fish of 40 kg and up, thawed slowly in salt water at the concentration of seawater so the flavor does not wash out; the salmon — one of the signature items — is thawed in ice water and cut piece by piece in-store every day. And the soy sauce: **five kinds sit at every table** (the lineup leans regional — a dashi soy sauce, a kelp soy sauce from Hokkaido, a sweet sashimi soy sauce, a rich dark one in the east or a sweet one in the west, and a ponzu), with a reduced-salt version free from the touch panel, so you can match the sauce to the fish — a small thing that sushi people genuinely appreciate. Regulars also point to the edges of the menu: a wide dessert case and more side dishes than the rivals carry. If Sushiro is the default and Kura is the event, Hama is the easy local.

## Manners and what not to do

- **Soy sauce goes on the fish, not the rice.** Dip the topping side — turn the piece over with your chopsticks and touch the fish to the sauce. Soaking the rice makes it fall apart and floods the flavor. A small puddle in the dish is enough; you can always add more.
- **Gari (pickled ginger) is a palate cleanser, not a topping.** Eat a slice or two *between* different fish to reset your taste. Piling it onto a piece of sushi is the equivalent of eating the garnish as a side dish — not forbidden, just a sign you may not have been told.
- **One direction only on the belt.** Take a plate as it passes, or let it go forever. Never put a plate back on the belt, never reach across to grab from the far side, and never touch a plate you are not taking. If you missed something, order it on the panel — it will arrive fresher anyway.
- **Your plate stack is your receipt.** Keep all finished plates at your seat (or feed them into the slot at Kura Sushi) until the bill is counted. Do not tidy them away, hand them to staff early, or stack another table's plates with yours.
- **Wasabi is adjustable, ginger is separate, and mixing is optional.** Stirring wasabi into your soy sauce is common in casual settings — at a chain nobody will mind — but know that the traditional form is a small dab of wasabi placed directly on the fish. Either way, start with less than you think.
- **Go easy on perfume.** Sushi is a food of delicate smells, and the person next to you is eating raw fish seasoned with almost nothing. Strong fragrance at a sushi counter — chain or high-end — is a genuine imposition. This is one of the most consistently mentioned points in Japanese dining etiquette.
- **No tipping.** As everywhere in Japan, staff cannot accept tips and coins left on the table become forgotten change to chase you with. The bill is the bill.
- **The hot-water tap can burn you — press it with the cup, never with your hand.** The water is close to boiling, and the metal tap itself gets hot enough to burn skin. The lever is designed to be pushed by the cup: hold the cup by its body, press the lever with the cup, and keep your fingers and your other hand clear. Do not let children work the tap alone. And do not use the powdered tea like a free souvenir — a cup or two across the meal is the normal rhythm.

## How locals actually choose

Habit first, as always: the chain nearest home with the shortest Saturday wait wins most weekends. Beyond that, the split is clear. Sushiro when you want the safe default and whatever limited item is on the posters this month. Kura Sushi when children are involved — the game is not a gimmick to a seven-year-old, it is the reason for the visit — or when the covered-lid system makes you more comfortable. Hama Sushi when the meal is as much about dessert as about fish, or when you want to play with the soy sauces.

Japanese families, it should be said, are not loyal the way gyudon fans are loyal — they drift between the three by queue length, parking, and which capsule series is running. Ask travelers, though, and you will start an argument. Sushiro defenders swear by the tuna and the limited items; Kura Sushi loyalists insist the game and the no-additives policy make it the only family answer; Hama Sushi fans point at the five soy sauces and the dessert case. The debate is real even where the local habit is not: these chains compete on systems and small pleasures, and the fish at the base price is closer than the marketing — or the arguments — suggest.
