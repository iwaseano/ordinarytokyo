Every neighborhood in Tokyo runs on cafe chains, and the chains are not interchangeable. One is where salarymen drink a coffee standing-adjacent in eight minutes before a train. One is a brick-walled time capsule where breakfast arrives free with your drink and nobody has hurried since 1968. One is the green logo you already know, doing a slightly different job here than it does at home. This page compares Doutor, Komeda, and Starbucks on the things that actually differ: how you order, what the coffee costs you in time and money, and which mood each one is for.

## What is Japanese cafe and kissaten culture?

Japan has two cafe traditions stacked on top of each other. The older one is the **kissaten** — the classic Japanese coffee house. Kissaten spread from the early 20th century and peaked in the postwar decades: dark wood, low light, velvet or brick, a siphon or a careful pour-over, coffee served in a real cup, and a room that treats staying a while as the entire point. The master often runs the shop alone. Nothing is fast and nothing is supposed to be.

The kissaten gave Japan two lasting customs. The first is coffee as a crafted, slightly formal drink — not fuel, an occasion. The second is the **morning service** (モーニング), born in Aichi, around Nagoya: order a coffee in the morning and toast, a boiled egg, and salad arrive with it at no extra charge. In Nagoya this was not a promotion. It was simply what a coffee house owed you.

The chains divided the inheritance between them. Doutor, founded in 1980, took the kissaten's coffee and rebuilt it for the station front: counter ordering, small prices, fast turnover — European cafe-bar speed with Japanese tidiness. Komeda, founded in Nagoya in 1968, kept the kissaten itself: brick walls, wooden beams, big soft seats, table service, and the morning service carried proudly into the chain era. Starbucks, arriving in 1996, imported the third-place idea and landed at the premium end — less a coffee purchase than a seat, a desk, and a view, rented by the cup.

Independent kissaten still exist and are worth seeking out on a second trip. But for a first trip, these three chains are where Japan actually drinks its daily coffee, and each one answers a different moment of the day.

## When locals reach for each one

- **"I have ten minutes and a train to catch"** — Doutor. It is deliberately placed at station fronts and business districts, and the whole system is tuned to get you in, caffeinated, and out.
- **"Slow morning, nowhere to be, feed me"** — Komeda. The morning service turns one coffee into breakfast, and the seats are built like your grandfather's armchair. Weekends fill with families doing exactly this.
- **"I need to sit with a laptop for two hours"** — Starbucks. You are paying partly for permission to stay, and everyone in the room understands the arrangement.
- **"A sandwich, not a snack"** — Doutor's Milano Sandwich at lunch, or Komeda when the group wants volume and nobody agrees on one dish.
- **"Coffee as a treat, not a routine"** — Komeda's Shiro-Noir, or a Japan-only seasonal drink at Starbucks.

If your mood is "just coffee, quickly, like a local on a weekday," you are in a Doutor mood. The rest of this page is for the other moods.

## Doutor: the station-front sprinter

Doutor is the everyday default for working Japan, and its design admits it: stores cluster at station fronts and office districts, counters run in a straight line, and a blend coffee costs about what a canned coffee plus a little more would. The coffee is straightforward and consistent — this is not where Tokyo comes for a tasting experience, and it does not pretend otherwise.

**The flow.** Order and pay at the counter. Hot drinks and sandwiches are handed over or called quickly; sandwiches like the Milano are assembled to order but fast. Take your tray, sit, eat, and return the tray to the return shelf on your way out. The whole visit can be eight minutes, and at morning peak, that is clearly the intended use.

**What to know.** The Milano Sandwich — ham, salami, and vegetables in a soft roll, in lettered variations — is the signature, and at lunch it outsells the coffee's supporting role entirely. Seating is compact; spreading out with luggage and a laptop marks you as someone who has mistaken Doutor for Starbucks, and the room will quietly judge the fit.

## Komeda: the kissaten that became a chain

Komeda Coffee, from Nagoya, is what happens when a classic coffee house refuses to modernize its soul and wins anyway. Brick exterior, warm wood inside, red velvet-style seats with high backs that give every table a semi-private feel. Staff bring the menu to you. Time moves differently here, on purpose.

**The flow.** Sit first. Order at the table — a server takes it, and the food comes to you. Pay at the register near the door when you leave. In the morning, order any drink and the **morning service** attaches itself: thick toast (with butter or jam) and an egg or a spread, at no extra charge, usually until 11:00. Locals plan around this; a Komeda breakfast is a recognized weekend institution for families and retirees alike.

**What to know.** Portion sizes are the running national joke, in your favor: dishes arrive visibly larger than their menu photos, the famous "reverse fraud." The **Shiro-Noir** — a warm, soft danish pastry crowned with soft-serve ice cream and syrup — is the signature dessert, and the regular size is a two-person decision even if the photo looks solo. There is a mini size; first-timers should take it seriously. Komeda is also the chain where groups linger longest, and the room is built to allow it — outside peak hours.

## Starbucks: the familiar logo, a different job

You know how Starbucks works, and in Japan the mechanics will not surprise you: counter order, your name or a mobile order, the same size ladder, reliable Wi-Fi. What differs is its position. In Japan, Starbucks is not the default daily coffee — Doutor holds that job at half the price. Starbucks is the premium room: architecturally distinctive stores, window seats designed for lingering, and an unspoken, widely honored license to work or study at your table.

**The flow.** Order and pay at the counter (mobile order is common and skips the line), pick up when called, and clear your cup to the return station when you leave. Many stores have outlets along the window counter — those seats go first, and regulars know it.

**What to know.** Japan-only seasonal drinks and food rotate constantly and are a genuine local enthusiasm, not just tourist bait; limited items sell out at popular stores. Reserve Roastery-level flagships aside, an ordinary neighborhood Starbucks in Tokyo is best understood as a pleasant, predictable workspace with good coffee — priced accordingly.

## Manners and what not to do

- **A seat follows a purchase, and one purchase has a time value.** Ordering before sitting is the rule at counter-style chains; at Komeda, sit and order promptly. Everywhere, a single drink honestly buys you a while — it does not buy you the whole afternoon at peak time.
- **Do not claim seats with luggage before ordering.** At Doutor and Starbucks, leaving bags on a table while you queue reads as seat-hoarding and draws real resentment in crowded stores. Order first, then sit. In a group, one person ordering while another waits *after* paying is the accepted version.
- **Outlets and Wi-Fi are a feature, not a promise.** Some Doutor stores have neither, and seats with outlets are a minority everywhere. If power matters, check or ask before you order — and a fully occupied outlet seat with a finished cup, at lunch peak, is the same faux pas as an empty bowl in a ramen shop.
- **No tipping, here as everywhere in Japan.** Cafe staff cannot accept it. The exact price and a "gochisousama deshita" or a simple thank-you completes the transaction.
- **Match your volume and duration to the room.** Kissaten-style spaces like Komeda are calm; calls belong outside. Doutor at 8 a.m. is a flow, not a lounge — eat, drink, and free the seat. Starbucks tolerates the longest stays, but a table of four held by one sleeping person is noticed everywhere in the world, including here.
- **Return your tray and cup.** Doutor has a return shelf; Starbucks has a return station. Komeda is the exception — leave the table as it is, staff clear it.

## How locals actually choose

Most people are not comparing cafes daily; they have a Doutor by their station, a Komeda near home, and a Starbucks they use as a meeting landmark. Beyond habit, the split is functional: Doutor is bought with the commute — speed and price, no decision required. Komeda is bought with time off — mornings, family breakfasts, long talks, and the specific pleasure of a room that refuses to hurry you. Starbucks is bought with a purpose — work to finish, a person to meet, a reliable seat in an unfamiliar neighborhood. The same person uses all three in the same week without feeling any contradiction, because in Japan they are not really competitors. They are shifts in the same day.
